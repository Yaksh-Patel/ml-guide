Optimization Algorithms
Gradient descent tells you which way is downhill. It says nothing about how far to step, and on a loss surface where curvature differs by orders of magnitude between directions, that omission is the whole problem. Every optimiser here is an answer to it — momentum accumulates a direction, adaptive methods give each parameter its own step size, and schedules change the answer over time.
Momentum: an average with a memory you can measure
Plain SGD treats each minibatch gradient as the truth and steps along it, so in a narrow valley it bounces between the walls and creeps along the floor. Momentum accumulates an exponentially weighted average of past gradients and steps along that:
$$v \leftarrow \beta v + (1-\beta)\nabla L, \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha v$$
The useful fact is that β has a concrete interpretation: the average spans roughly 1/(1−β) recent gradients.
βEffective averaging windowAmplification in a consistent direction
0.9 (standard)~10 stepsup to 10× the single-gradient step
0.99~100 stepsup to 100×
0.999~1,000 stepsup to 1,000×
That explains both of momentum's effects at once. Components that agree across steps — the valley floor — accumulate and get amplified up to 1/(1−β) times. Components that alternate — the walls — cancel in the average. So it simultaneously accelerates progress and damps oscillation, which is why it is not a heuristic bolted onto SGD but a strict improvement, and why β = 0.9 with 1/(1−β) = 10 is the near-universal default.
It also explains a common surprise: raising β requires lowering the learning rate. The effective step is roughly α/(1−β), so moving β from 0.9 to 0.99 multiplies the effective step by 10, and keeping α fixed usually diverges.
Optimiser paths down an ill-conditioned valley
Adam, and why its step size is unitless
Adam keeps two exponential averages per parameter — the mean of the gradient and the mean of its square — and divides one by the square root of the other:
$$m \leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1-\beta_1)g, \qquad v \leftarrow \beta_2 v + (1-\beta_2)g^2, \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha\,\frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat v} + \varepsilon}$$
Look at the units of that ratio. The numerator is a gradient; the denominator is the root-mean-square of a gradient. They cancel — so m̂/√v̂ is dimensionless, of order 1, regardless of how large the gradients are. The actual step taken is therefore about α, always.
That single property explains most of Adam's reputation. It means α transfers across architectures, layers and loss scales, where SGD's does not — a layer with tiny gradients still moves, and a layer with huge gradients does not explode. It is why 1e-3 is a usable default across wildly different models, and why Adam handles sparse gradients well: a rarely-seen embedding row has a small v, so its infrequent gradients are scaled up rather than lost.
It also explains the cost. Adam stores m and v per parameter, so training state is four times the model:

That table is the entire reason 8-bit optimisers, optimiser-state sharding (ZeRO/FSDP) and mixed precision exist. When people say a 7B model "needs" far more memory than its weights suggest, this is what they mean — and SGD with momentum, storing one extra buffer instead of two, is meaningfully cheaper.
Bias correction, and the number that justifies it
Both averages start at zero, so early on they are badly biased toward zero — they are averages that have not yet seen enough terms. Adam divides them by (1 − βᵗ) to correct for exactly this, and the size of the correction is worth seeing:
$$\hat m = \frac{m}{1 - \beta_1^{t}}, \qquad \hat v = \frac{v}{1 - \beta_2^{t}}$$
Step t1 − β₂ᵗ (β₂ = 0.999)√v̂ correction factorUncorrected step would be
10.00131.6×31.6× too large
100.01010.0×10× too large
1000.0953.2×3.2× too large
1,0000.6321.26×26% too large
At step 1, v is a thousandth of its proper size, so √v is about 31.6× too small — and since it sits in the denominator, the first step would be 31.6× larger than intended. Without correction, Adam's opening move is a wild jump from a randomly initialised network. The correction is not a refinement; it is what stops the first hundred steps from being destructive.
and this is also why warmup exists

Bias correction fixes the expected value of v̂ but not its variance. Early on, v̂ is estimated from a handful of gradients, so it is not merely small — it is unreliable, and dividing by the square root of an unreliable number produces erratic step sizes exactly when the model is most fragile.

That is the mechanistic argument for linear warmup: ramp α from ~0 over the first 1–5% of steps so that by the time full-size steps are taken, v̂ has accumulated enough samples to be trustworthy. It is why transformer training essentially always warms up while SGD-trained convnets usually do not — SGD has no adaptive denominator to get wrong. RAdam attacks the same problem by rectifying the variance directly rather than by scheduling around it.

Choosing, and the settings that are not defaults
The honest summary is that optimiser choice is architecture-dependent and the differences are small next to the learning rate.
  • AdamW for transformers, and not plain Adam — routing L2 through the loss lets the adaptive denominator divide the penalty, so the best-signalled parameters get the least decay. Exclude biases and normalisation gains from decay, as every reference implementation does
  • SGD with momentum plus cosine annealing for convnets. Well-tuned, it usually matches or beats Adam on final vision accuracy while using less memory. Adam gets to a decent loss faster; SGD often finishes better, which matters when you care about the last half-percent rather than the first epoch
  • β₂ = 0.95, not 0.999, for large language models. This is the non-obvious one, from the GPT-3 recipe and now standard. The default 0.999 averages over ~1,000 steps, which is too slow to react when the gradient distribution shifts during a long run; 0.95 averages over ~20 and adapts faster. Knowing that the published default is not what large runs use is a real signal
  • Gradient clipping at global norm 1.0 before the optimiser step. Standard in every large run, because one anomalous batch can otherwise corrupt the state of a model that has been training for days
  • Differential learning rates when fine-tuning — a small rate for pretrained layers, a larger one for a fresh head. A single global rate either fails to train the head or destroys the pretrained representation
On the generalisation gap: the frequently repeated claim is that Adam finds worse minima than SGD. Treat it carefully. The gap is real and reproducible on image classification, largely absent on transformers, and much of it closes when the two are tuned equally hard and given comparable weight decay — a significant part of the original gap was Adam's broken L2, which AdamW fixed. Say it is architecture-dependent rather than a law.

Mistakes that survive code review
  • Not tuning the learning rate because the optimiser is adaptive. Adam makes α transferable, not irrelevant. It remains the most impactful hyperparameter by a wide margin
  • Using Adam's weight_decay and calling it L2. Use AdamW; the two are not equivalent and the difference grows with scale
  • No warmup on a transformer. Full-size steps while v̂ is estimated from a handful of gradients, on a randomly initialised model. Loss spikes or NaN in the first few hundred steps
  • Raising momentum without lowering α. The effective step is α/(1−β), so 0.9 → 0.99 multiplies it by ten
  • Leaving β₂ at 0.999 for a long run. Adapts too slowly as the gradient distribution shifts. Large-model recipes use 0.95
  • No gradient clipping. One bad batch can destroy days of training. Clip by global norm so direction survives
  • Comparing optimisers at one learning rate each. Every optimiser has its own optimal α, often orders of magnitude apart. A fair comparison tunes α for each; otherwise you are measuring which default happened to suit your problem
  • Forgetting that the scheduler needs stepping — and at the right granularity. A per-step schedule stepped once per epoch decays hundreds of times too slowly, and nothing errors
  • Reusing an LR after changing the batch size. Larger batches give less noisy gradients and support larger steps; the linear-scaling rule is the usual starting point
What large runs actually use
  • The standard LLM recipe is remarkably fixed: AdamW, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.95, linear warmup over a small percentage of steps, then cosine decay to roughly 10% of peak, gradient clipping at 1.0, weight decay ~0.1 excluding biases and norms. It has held across model families, which makes deviating from it a decision to justify rather than a default
  • Optimiser state is a distributed-systems problem. ZeRO and FSDP shard m and v across devices precisely because they are twice the model. Choosing Adafactor or 8-bit Adam is a memory decision, not an optimisation one
  • Vision splits by architecture, not by fashion. Convnets still train well with SGD, momentum and cosine annealing; vision transformers want AdamW. That the same task has different best optimisers for different architectures is the clearest evidence there is no universal winner
  • Learning-rate range tests are standard practice. Sweep α upward over a few hundred steps and plot the loss: the usable maximum is just before it turns up. Minutes of compute, and far more reliable than guessing or inheriting someone else's number
  • Fine-tuning uses parameter groups. Backbone at a low rate, new head an order of magnitude higher, sometimes with layerwise decay across depth. The Trainer APIs expose this because a single global rate is usually wrong for both parts
🎯 In an interview

Explain momentum with the window. "It is an exponential moving average over roughly 1/(1−β) gradients — ten at β = 0.9. Consistent directions accumulate and get amplified up to 10×, alternating ones cancel, so it accelerates and damps at the same time." Then the consequence most people miss: the effective step is α/(1−β), so raising β means lowering α.

Explain Adam by its units. "m̂/√v̂ is a gradient divided by the RMS of a gradient, so it is dimensionless and of order 1 — the step is about α no matter how large the gradients are. That is why α transfers across architectures and why sparse embeddings still get updates." A far better answer than "it adapts the learning rate per parameter".

Expect: "Why does Adam need bias correction, and why warmup?" — At t = 1, v is a thousandth of its proper size, so √v is 31.6× too small and the first step would be 31.6× too large. Correction fixes the mean; warmup fixes the variance, because v̂ from a handful of gradients is unreliable and you are dividing by its square root. That distinction — mean versus variance — is the answer that stands out.

Two things worth volunteering: that large runs use β₂ = 0.95 rather than the 0.999 default, because 0.999 averages over ~1,000 steps and adapts too slowly; and that Adam's state is two buffers per parameter, so a 7B model in fp32 carries 112 GB of weights, gradients and optimiser state — which is why sharding and 8-bit optimisers exist. Be careful with "Adam generalises worse than SGD": true and reproducible on convnets, largely absent on transformers, and partly an artefact of Adam's broken L2 that AdamW fixed.
