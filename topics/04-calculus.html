Calculus & Optimization
You never compute a derivative by hand in ML — a framework does it. What you do need is the ability to read a gradient and say why training stalled, exploded, or converged to something useless. That reading is what this topic is for.
Derivatives: a local slope, nothing more
The derivative at a point answers one question: if I nudge the input a little, how much does the output move, and in which direction? Everything else is bookkeeping.
$$\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \\ \tfrac{d}{dx}x^n &= n x^{n-1} \qquad \tfrac{d}{dx}e^x = e^x \qquad \tfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \tfrac{1}{x} \\ \sigma'(x) &= \sigma(x)\,(1 - \sigma(x)) \\ \text{ReLU}'(x) &= \begin{cases} 1 & x > 0 \\ 0 & x \le 0 \end{cases} \end{aligned}$$
Those last two are the ones to actually remember. The sigmoid derivative peaks at 0.25 and decays to nothing at both ends — that single fact is the vanishing-gradient problem. The ReLU derivative is exactly 1 or exactly 0, which is why ReLU trains deep networks and why a unit can die permanently.
The gradient: one number per parameter
With many inputs, the derivative becomes a vector of partial derivatives — one per parameter, each computed while holding the others fixed. It points in the direction of steepest increase, which is why every update subtracts it.
$$\nabla f = \left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}, \ldots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\right]^{T} \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \nabla L(\theta)$$
A model with 7 billion parameters has a gradient with 7 billion components. Each one is a scalar answering "if I nudge only this weight, how does the loss move?" — and they are all computed in a single backward pass.
what the gradient norm tells you

‖∇L‖ is the single most useful number to log during training. Growing without bound: exploding gradients, clip them. Collapsing to ~0 while loss is still high: vanishing gradients or dead units. Oscillating wildly between steps: learning rate too high. You can diagnose most training failures from that one curve.

The chain rule is backpropagation
A neural network is a composition of functions. The chain rule says the derivative of a composition is the product of the derivatives along the path — so you can compute a gradient for a 100-layer network by multiplying 100 local derivatives together.
$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial a_3} \cdot \frac{\partial a_3}{\partial a_2} \cdot \frac{\partial a_2}{\partial a_1} \cdot \frac{\partial a_1}{\partial w_1}$$
Because it is a product, depth is dangerous. Multiply many numbers below 1 and the result vanishes; multiply many above 1 and it explodes. Both failures are the same arithmetic seen from opposite ends.
worked — why sigmoid stopped being used in hidden layers

Sigmoid's derivative maxes out at 0.25. Ten sigmoid layers, all at their best case, give 0.25¹⁰ ≈ 9.5 × 10⁻⁷. The gradient reaching layer one is a millionth of the gradient at the output, so the early layers effectively never train. ReLU's derivative is 1 on the active side, so 1¹⁰ = 1 and the signal survives. That is the entire argument, and it is arithmetic rather than intuition.

Chain Rule: gradient flows backward
Convexity: whether "the" minimum exists
A convex function has exactly one basin. Any local minimum is the global one, so gradient descent cannot get stuck in a bad place — only converge slowly.
$$f''(x) \ge 0 \quad \text{(1-D)} \qquad \nabla^2 f \succeq 0 \quad \text{(Hessian is PSD)}$$
  • Convex: linear and ridge regression, logistic regression, SVM with a fixed kernel, lasso. Reproducible answers; the seed does not matter
  • Not convex: every neural network. Different seeds give genuinely different models
The reassuring result is that in very high dimensions, bad local minima are rare — most critical points are saddles, and a saddle has a descent direction to escape along. That is why non-convexity turned out to be survivable in practice.
Second-order information, and why nobody uses it
The Hessian holds all second derivatives and describes curvature: whether the surface is a narrow ravine or a round bowl. Newton's method uses it to take a perfectly scaled step.
$$\theta \leftarrow \theta - (\nabla^2 L)^{-1} \nabla L$$
For n parameters the Hessian has n² entries. At 7 billion parameters that is 4.9 × 10¹⁹ numbers, so it is not stored, let alone inverted. Adam is a cheap stand-in: it keeps a running average of squared gradients per coordinate, which approximates the diagonal of the curvature and costs two extra vectors instead of a matrix.
This is also the honest answer to "why not use second-order methods": you cannot afford them, and the diagonal approximation captures most of the benefit.
The tricks that actually come up
TrickWhy it exists
Log-sum-expexp() overflows on large logits. Subtract the max first — mathematically identical, numerically safe. Every softmax implementation does this

JacobianGradient generalised to vector-valued outputs. Autodiff computes Jacobian-vector products without ever materialising the matrix
Mistakes that produce plausible, wrong training
  • Forgetting the negative sign. The gradient points uphill. Descent subtracts it. Add it instead and the loss climbs smoothly — which looks like a bug in the data, not the sign
  • Not zeroing gradients. In PyTorch, .backward() accumulates. Skip optimizer.zero_grad() and every step uses the sum of all gradients so far. Training appears to work for a few steps, then diverges
  • Treating the learning rate as a minor knob. It is the most consequential hyperparameter in deep learning by a wide margin. Too high and you bounce out of the basin; too low and you converge to the nearest mediocre point
  • Assuming a differentiable loss where there is none. Accuracy, F1 and AUC have zero gradient almost everywhere — you cannot optimise them directly. That is precisely why cross-entropy exists as a differentiable surrogate
  • Ignoring the ReLU kink. ReLU is not differentiable at exactly 0. Frameworks pick a subgradient (usually 0) and move on. It is harmless in practice, but claiming ReLU is differentiable everywhere is a quick way to look careless
Where this shows up in real systems
  • Autodiff is the whole framework. PyTorch records a graph of operations during the forward pass, then walks it backwards applying the chain rule. Nothing symbolic happens; it is bookkeeping plus a table of local derivatives
  • Learning-rate schedules encode curvature intuition. Warmup exists because early gradients are large and badly scaled; cosine decay exists because near a minimum you want small steps. Both are calculus arguments dressed as recipes
  • Mixed precision needs loss scaling. fp16 gradients underflow to zero, so the loss is multiplied by a large constant before the backward pass and the gradients divided afterwards — linearity of differentiation, exploited deliberately
  • The Adam variance term is diagonal curvature. Dividing by √v̂ gives each coordinate its own effective step size, which is a cheap Newton method restricted to the diagonal
🎯 In an interview

The question is almost always the chain rule. Say that backpropagation is the chain rule applied to a computation graph, that the gradient is a product of local derivatives, and that being a product is exactly why deep networks vanish or explode. Then give the 0.25¹⁰ number — it converts a vague claim into a demonstration.

Expect: "Why not use Newton's method?" — The Hessian is n × n. Quote the number for a real model size and note that Adam approximates its diagonal for two extra vectors.

Also common: "Why can't we optimise accuracy directly?" — Zero gradient almost everywhere; cross-entropy is the differentiable surrogate.

Strong close: mention that you log the gradient norm during training and what each failure shape looks like. It signals you have debugged a real training run rather than read about one.

In code
Gradient descent from scratch on a convex problem, then the same gradient obtained by autodiff. Seeing the two agree is what makes backpropagation stop feeling like magic.
import numpy as np

# Ridge objective: J(w) = ||Xw - y||^2 / n + lam * ||w||^2
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X, w_true = rng.normal(size=(400, 3)), np.array([1.5, -2.0, 0.5])
y = X @ w_true + 0.3 * rng.normal(size=400)
lam = 0.1

def loss_and_grad(w):
    r = X @ w - y
    J = r @ r / len(y) + lam * w @ w
    g = 2 * X.T @ r / len(y) + 2 * lam * w          # analytic gradient
    return J, g

w, lr = np.zeros(3), 0.1
for step in range(200):
    J, g = loss_and_grad(w)
    w -= lr * g
    if step % 50 == 0:
        print(f"step {step:3d}  J={J:.4f}  |g|={np.linalg.norm(g):.4f}")

# Numerical check: the single most useful debugging trick in ML
eps = 1e-6
num = np.array([(loss_and_grad(w + eps*e)[0] - loss_and_grad(w - eps*e)[0]) / (2*eps)
                for e in np.eye(3)])
print("analytic vs numeric:", np.allclose(loss_and_grad(w)[1], num, atol=1e-5))
