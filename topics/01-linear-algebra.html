Linear Algebra
Not a prerequisite you clear and forget — the notation ML is written in. A dataset is a matrix, a layer is a matrix product, a similarity is a dot product, and a regulariser is a norm. Once these four sentences read as literal rather than metaphorical, most ML papers become parseable.
Vectors: one row of your data
A vector is an ordered list of numbers, and in practice it is one row of your dataframe. A customer with age 34, balance 1200 and 3 accounts is the vector [34, 1200, 3]. Two operations on vectors carry almost all the weight in ML: length, and the dot product.
$$\begin{aligned} \|\mathbf{v}\|_2 &= \sqrt{v_1^2 + \cdots + v_n^2} \quad \text{(length — Euclidean, or L2)} \\ \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} &= \sum_i u_i v_i = \|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta \end{aligned}$$
That second identity is the useful one, because it says the dot product measures alignment. Read it right to left: if you divide out both lengths, what remains is the cosine of the angle between them.
worked

u = [3, 4], v = [4, 3]. Dot product 3·4 + 4·3 = 24. Both have length 5, so cos θ = 24/25 = 0.96 — about 16° apart. Now v = [−4, 3]: dot product is −12 + 12 = 0, so they are exactly perpendicular. Zero dot product means the two features share no direction at all.

  • Cosine similarity is the dot product of normalised vectors — which is why every embedding search normalises first, then uses a plain dot product
  • Magnitude often encodes frequency, not meaning — in text embeddings a common word gets a longer vector. Compare angles, not distances, unless you know why you want distance
Matrices, and the shape discipline that prevents most bugs
A matrix is a grid of numbers, and your dataset X is an (n × d) one: n rows of samples, d columns of features. A matrix product is the only operation you need to be fluent in, and the rule is entirely mechanical — the inner dimensions must match, and they vanish.
$$\underbrace{A}_{m \times k} \; \underbrace{B}_{k \times n} \; = \; \underbrace{C}_{m \times n} \qquad (AB)_{ij} = \sum_{p} A_{ip} B_{pj}$$
A neural network layer is literally X @ W + b: an (n × d) batch times a (d × h) weight matrix gives (n × h) activations. Stack three of those and you have described a whole MLP forward pass.
the habit worth building

Write the shape next to every line as you go — # (32, 768). Most deep-learning bugs are a transpose or a broadcast in the wrong place, and they do not raise an error: they raise a loss curve that never moves. Shapes in comments turn a two-hour debug into a ten-second read.

  • AB ≠ BA. Order is meaning. Reversing two layers is not a refactor
  • Transpose flips the shape, so Xᵀ X is (d × d) — feature-by-feature — while X Xᵀ is (n × n) — sample-by-sample. The first is a covariance; the second is a Gram matrix, and on a large dataset it will not fit in memory
Eigenvectors: the directions a transform does not turn
Most vectors, pushed through a matrix, come out pointing somewhere else. A few come out pointing the same way, only longer or shorter. Those are the eigenvectors, and how much they stretch is the eigenvalue.
$$A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \det(A - \lambda I) = 0$$
worked

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, solving det(A − λI) = (2−λ)² − 1 = 0 gives λ = 3 and λ = 1. The λ = 3 eigenvector is [1, 1]: A·[1,1] = [3,3], the same direction, three times as long. The λ = 1 eigenvector is [1, −1], which A leaves completely unchanged. Trace 4 = 3 + 1, determinant 3 = 3 × 1 — both checks always hold, and they are the fastest way to catch an arithmetic slip.

Why ML cares: take the eigenvectors of a covariance matrix and you have the directions of greatest variance in your data, ordered by eigenvalue. That is not an analogy for PCA — it is PCA. The largest eigenvalue is the variance along the first principal component.
Eigenvector: only scaled, not rotated
SVD: the decomposition that always exists
Eigendecomposition needs a square matrix. Your data almost never is. SVD factors any matrix into a rotation, a scaling, and another rotation.
$$A = U \Sigma V^{T}$$
  • Σ holds the singular values, largest first — how much of the matrix lives in each direction
  • Truncate to the top k and you have the best possible rank-k approximation. That single fact is PCA, LSA, and classical recommender matrix factorisation
  • Related to eigenvalues by σᵢ² = λᵢ of AᵀA — same information, obtained without ever forming AᵀA
Norms decide what your regulariser punishes
$$\|\mathbf{w}\|_1 = \sum_i |w_i| \qquad \|\mathbf{w}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_i w_i^2}$$
The geometry explains the behaviour everyone memorises. An L2 ball is round, so a loss contour touching it lands almost anywhere — coefficients shrink but stay non-zero. An L1 ball is a diamond with corners on the axes, so the contour usually touches a corner, and a corner means some coefficient is exactly zero.
L1 selects because its ball has corners. That is the whole reason, and it is worth saying out loud rather than reciting "L1 induces sparsity".

Mistakes that cost real time
  • Inverting a matrix to solve a system. np.linalg.inv(A) @ b is slower and numerically worse than np.linalg.solve(A, b). If you find yourself typing inv, you almost always want solve or lstsq
  • Forming XᵀX when SVD would do. Squaring a matrix squares its condition number, so a mildly ill-conditioned X becomes a badly ill-conditioned XᵀX. This is exactly why sklearn's PCA runs SVD on X instead of eigendecomposing the covariance
  • Confusing eigenvalues with singular values. Eigen needs square; SVD does not. They coincide only for symmetric positive-semidefinite matrices — which covariance matrices happen to be, hence the confusion
  • Silent broadcasting. Adding a (3, 1) to a (1, 4) gives you a (3, 4) and no warning. Adding a (32,) bias to a (32, 10) activation broadcasts down the wrong axis and trains to a plausible-looking, wrong model
  • Ignoring rank. A rank-deficient XᵀX means your features are linearly dependent — one is a combination of the others. OLS has no unique answer, and the coefficients you get are arbitrary. Check matrix_rank before trusting any coefficient interpretation
At production scale
  • Hardware is built for one operation. A TPU's matrix multiply unit and a GPU's tensor cores exist because transformer training is overwhelmingly dense matrix products. Model architecture choices are often really choices about what maps well onto that unit
  • Attention's 1/√d_k is variance control. The dot product of two d-dimensional random vectors has variance proportional to d, so for large d the logits spread out and softmax saturates into a near one-hot with vanishing gradients. Dividing by √d_k renormalises it — a linear-algebra fix to a training-stability problem
  • LoRA is a rank argument. Fine-tuning updates are empirically low-rank, so you can learn ΔW = BA with B and A skinny instead of updating all of W. Millions of parameters instead of billions, from one observation about rank
  • Recommenders factorise. A user–item matrix is mostly missing, so you fit user and item embedding matrices whose product reconstructs the observed entries. A recommendation is then a dot product
🎯 In an interview

If asked why linear algebra matters: be concrete rather than reverent. "A forward pass is a chain of matrix products. Attention is a scaled dot product. PCA is the eigendecomposition of a covariance matrix. Ridge and lasso differ only in which norm they penalise." Four sentences, four load-bearing facts.

Expect: "Why does PCA use SVD instead of eigendecomposition?" — Because forming XᵀX squares the condition number and loses precision; SVD gets the same singular directions straight from X. Say the words "condition number" and the question is over.

Also common: "Why divide attention scores by √d_k?" — Variance of the dot product grows with dimension; without the scaling, softmax saturates and gradients vanish.

Do not say that matrix multiplication is commutative, or that you would invert a matrix to solve a linear system. Both are quick, memorable ways to lose the room.

In code
Everything in ML reduces to these four operations. Note that eigenvectors of the covariance matrix are exactly what PCA returns — the same object, two names.
import numpy as np

X = np.random.randn(200, 5)                 # 200 samples, 5 features

# 1. Projection: least-squares solution via the normal equations
y = X @ np.array([2., -1., 0.5, 0., 3.]) + 0.1 * np.random.randn(200)
beta = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)    # never invert; solve

# 2. Eigendecomposition of the covariance = PCA directions
C = np.cov(X, rowvar=False)                 # 5x5, symmetric PSD
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(C)              # eigh for symmetric: faster, stable
order = vals.argsort()[::-1]
vals, vecs = vals[order], vecs[:, order]

# 3. SVD: works even when X.T @ X is singular
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False)
assert np.allclose(vals, S**2 / (len(X) - 1))   # same spectrum, better conditioned

# 4. Rank and conditioning tell you when a solve will misbehave
print(np.linalg.matrix_rank(X), np.linalg.cond(X).round(2))
