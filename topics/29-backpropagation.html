Backpropagation & Training Loop
Backpropagation is not a formula to memorise — it is the observation that all the derivatives a network needs share almost all of their work, so you can compute every one of them in a single backward sweep instead of one pass per parameter. That reuse is the difference between a billion-parameter model being trainable and being arithmetically impossible.
Why backprop exists: a factor of a billion
Suppose you wanted the gradient without it. The obvious approach is finite differences: nudge one parameter, run the network, see how the loss changed, repeat.
$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_i} \approx \frac{L(w_i + \epsilon) - L(w_i - \epsilon)}{2\epsilon}$$
That is two forward passes per parameter. For a model with 10⁹ parameters, one gradient step costs 2 × 10⁹ forward passes. Backpropagation computes the same 10⁹ derivatives in one backward pass, costing roughly twice a forward pass.
MethodCost of one full gradientFor 10⁹ parameters
Finite differences2P forward passes2,000,000,000 forward passes
Backpropagation≈ 2 forward passes, total2
That ratio — around 10⁹ — is why deep learning is possible at all. Nothing else in the field buys a factor like it, and it comes not from an approximation but from noticing that the derivative with respect to an early weight contains the derivative with respect to every later one. Compute the late ones first, cache them, and reuse. Backprop is dynamic programming applied to the chain rule.
Finite differences do keep one job: gradient checking. When you implement a custom layer or loss, compare its analytic gradient against a finite-difference estimate on a couple of parameters. It is far too slow to train with and exactly right for confirming you got the maths correct.
Backprop: gradients flow backward layer by layer via chain rule
The chain rule, read as a data structure
Every network is a directed graph of elementary operations. The forward pass evaluates the graph and — crucially — caches the intermediate values. The backward pass walks the same graph in reverse, and at each node multiplies the gradient arriving from above by that node's local derivative.
$$\underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial w^{(1)}}}_{\text{what you want}} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y}}_{\text{from the loss}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial \hat y}{\partial h^{(2)}} \cdot \frac{\partial h^{(2)}}{\partial h^{(1)}}}_{\text{shared by every earlier layer}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial h^{(1)}}{\partial w^{(1)}}}_{\text{local}}$$
Look at the middle bracket: every parameter below layer 1 needs that exact product. Computing it once and passing it down is the whole trick. In implementations that shared quantity is the error signal δ, and the backward pass is just δ being handed from layer to layer, each layer multiplying in its own local Jacobian.
Two practical consequences of "the forward pass caches values":
  • Memory scales with activations, not parameters. Training needs every intermediate value kept alive until its gradient is consumed, which is why a model that infers comfortably can fail to train on the same GPU. Gradient checkpointing trades compute for memory by recomputing activations instead of storing them
  • Anything that breaks the graph breaks learning silently. An in-place operation on a cached tensor, a .detach(), a conversion to NumPy and back — the forward pass still produces a number, the loss still decreases or does not, and the gradient is simply wrong or absent. This is the most common category of deep learning bug, and it never raises an error
Concrete 2-Layer Gradient Computation (Step-by-Step)
$$\begin{aligned} \text{Architecture: } x\,(2D) \rightarrow h\,(3D,\text{ReLU}) \rightarrow \hat{y}\,(1D,\text{sigmoid}) \\ \textbf{--- OUTPUT LAYER ---} \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} = \frac{\hat{y}-y}{\hat{y}(1-\hat{y})} \quad \text{(BCE w.r.t. } \hat{y}\text{)} \\ \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z^2} = \hat{y}(1-\hat{y}) \quad \text{(sigmoid derivative)} \\ \delta^2 = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z^2} = \hat{y}-y \quad \text{[these cancel nicely!]} \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial W^2} = \delta^2 \cdot (h^1)^T \quad \text{(outer product)} \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial b^2} = \delta^2 \\ \textbf{--- HIDDEN LAYER ---} \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial h^1} = (W^2)^T \cdot \delta^2 \quad \text{[}W^{2T}\text{ routes gradient back]} \\ \frac{\partial h^1}{\partial z^1} = \mathbb{1}(z^1>0) \quad \text{(ReLU: 1 if positive, 0 if not)} \\ \delta^1 = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z^1} = (W^2)^T\cdot\delta^2 \odot \mathbb{1}(z^1>0) \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial W^1} = \delta^1 \cdot x^T \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial b^1} = \delta^1 \\ \textbf{--- WEIGHT UPDATE ---} \\ W^2 \leftarrow W^2 - \alpha \cdot \frac{\partial L}{\partial W^2} \\ W^1 \leftarrow W^1 - \alpha \cdot \frac{\partial L}{\partial W^1} \end{aligned}$$
Gradient descent: loss surface + parameter update steps
Vanishing and exploding gradients are one phenomenon
The gradient reaching layer 1 is a product of per-layer factors — each roughly the layer's weight matrix times its activation derivative. A product of L numbers is a knife edge: if the typical factor is even slightly off 1, the result is exponential in depth.
$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial h^{(1)}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h^{(L)}} \prod_{k=2}^{L} \underbrace{\big(W^{(k)}\big)^{T} \odot \phi'\big(z^{(k)}\big)}_{\text{one factor per layer}}$$
Put a single number on each factor and take 50 layers:
Per-layer factorGradient after 50 layersWhat happens
0.81.4 × 10⁻⁵early layers never move — vanishing
0.95.2 × 10⁻³early layers learn ~200× slower than late ones
1.01.0the only stable value
1.1117large, unstable updates
1.29,100NaN within a few steps — exploding
A ±20% deviation per layer spans nearly nine orders of magnitude over 50 layers. So vanishing and exploding are not two separate problems with two separate fixes — they are the same instability, and every remedy in deep learning is an attempt to hold that per-layer factor near 1:
  • Initialisation sets the factor to ≈1 at step zero — that is precisely what He and Xavier scaling compute
  • Normalisation layers re-impose unit scale at every layer, every step, so drift cannot accumulate
  • Residual connections add an identity path, so the factor is (1 + something) rather than something — the gradient has a route that multiplies by exactly 1. This, more than depth itself, is what made 100-layer networks trainable
  • ReLU-family activations contribute a derivative of 1 rather than ≤ 0.25
  • Gradient clipping handles the exploding side directly: if ‖g‖ exceeds a threshold, rescale the whole gradient to that norm. At ‖g‖ = 100 with a threshold of 1, every component is multiplied by 0.01 — the direction is preserved and only the length is capped, which is why clipping by global norm is preferred over clipping element-wise
what you would actually observe

Vanishing: log the gradient norm per layer. A healthy network shows norms within an order of magnitude of each other; a vanishing one shows the first layers orders of magnitude below the last. Symptom in training: the loss falls a little and then flattens, and freezing the early layers changes nothing because they were not learning anyway.

Exploding: loss spikes to a huge value or NaN, usually within the first few hundred steps, and often after one bad batch. Print the global gradient norm every step during early training — a norm that jumps by orders of magnitude tells you immediately that you need clipping or a lower learning rate, and distinguishes this from a data bug.

Normalisation, and a correction to the usual story
Batch normalisation standardises each activation across the batch, then applies a learned scale and shift so the network can undo it if that helps:
$$\hat z = \frac{z - \mu_{\text{batch}}}{\sqrt{\sigma^2_{\text{batch}} + \epsilon}}, \qquad \text{out} = \gamma \hat z + \beta$$
It made deep networks dramatically easier to train — higher learning rates, faster convergence, less sensitivity to initialisation. The explanation in the original paper, "reducing internal covariate shift", is now believed to be wrong. Santurkar et al. showed you can inject covariate shift back in after normalising and keep the benefit; what BatchNorm actually does is smooth the loss landscape, making gradients more predictive so larger steps stay safe. Worth knowing, because the covariate-shift story is still repeated as fact and being able to correct it is a genuine signal.
The practical details matter more than the theory:
  • It behaves differently in training and inference. Training uses batch statistics; inference uses running averages accumulated during training. Forgetting model.eval() means inference uses whatever batch happens to be present — so predictions depend on what else is in the request. A genuinely nasty production bug, and a common one
  • It breaks at small batch size. Batch statistics from four samples are noise. Below roughly 16, use GroupNorm or LayerNorm instead
  • LayerNorm normalises across features within one sample, so it is batch-size independent and has no train/inference discrepancy. That is why every transformer uses it — with variable-length sequences and batch size 1 at inference, BatchNorm is simply unusable
  • It interacts badly with dropout. Dropout changes activation variance between training and inference, which is exactly the statistic BatchNorm estimated. Modern architectures generally pick one
The Complete Training Loop
🐍 Python
for epoch in range(num_epochs):
    for X_batch, y_batch in dataloader:        # Step 1: mini-batch
        # --- FORWARD PASS ---
        z1 = X_batch @ W1 + b1                 # Step 2a: linear transform
        h1 = relu(z1)                           # Step 2b: non-linearity
        z2 = h1 @ W2 + b2                       # Step 2c: output layer
        y_hat = sigmoid(z2)                     # Step 2d: probability

        # --- LOSS ---
        loss = binary_cross_entropy(y_hat, y_batch)   # Step 3: scalar loss

        # --- BACKWARD PASS ---
        optimizer.zero_grad()                   # Step 4a: clear old gradients!
        loss.backward()                         # Step 4b: compute all ∂L/∂W

        # --- CLIP (optional but critical for RNNs/Transformers) ---
        clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)  # Step 5

        # --- UPDATE ---
        optimizer.step()                        # Step 6: W ← W − α∇L

    # --- VALIDATION ---
    model.eval()
    with torch.no_grad():                       # Step 7: no grad tracking
        val_loss = evaluate(model, val_loader)
    model.train()

What Autograd Does For You
In PyTorch, loss.backward() executes the entire chain automatically by traversing the computation graph in reverse. Each operation records its local gradient during the forward pass, then multiplies them in chain-rule order during backward.
🐍 Python
x = torch.tensor([...], requires_grad=True)
z1 = x @ W1 + b1          # records: ∂z1/∂W1 = x
h  = z1.clamp(min=0)       # records: ∂h/∂z1 = 1(z1>0)
z2 = h @ W2 + b2
loss = F.binary_cross_entropy(z2.sigmoid(), y)
loss.backward()            # traverses graph, accumulates grads
print(W1.grad)             # ∂L/∂W1 is now populated
Mistakes that survive code review
  • Forgetting optimizer.zero_grad(). PyTorch accumulates gradients by default, so without it step k uses the sum of all gradients so far. Training appears to work and converges to nonsense. The single most common beginner bug, and it is silent
  • Forgetting model.eval(). Leaves dropout active and BatchNorm using batch statistics at inference, so predictions become noisy and batch-dependent. Its mirror image — forgetting model.train() after validating — silently disables your regularisation for the rest of training
  • Breaking the graph. An in-place edit, a stray .detach(), a round trip through NumPy. No error, wrong or absent gradients
  • Calling .item() or storing the loss tensor for logging. Keeping the tensor keeps the entire graph alive, so memory grows every step until it dies. Log loss.item(), not loss
  • No gradient clipping on recurrent or transformer training. Cheap insurance against a single bad batch taking down a long run. Clip by global norm so the direction survives
  • Tuning anything before the learning rate. It dominates every other hyperparameter. An architecture search at the wrong learning rate measures the learning rate
  • Not checking that the model can overfit ten samples. If it cannot drive the loss to near zero on a tiny batch, the problem is wiring — misaligned labels, a frozen layer, a detached graph — and no tuning will help
  • Reading the training loss as progress. It falls almost by construction. The validation curve carries the information
  • Assuming a bigger batch is strictly better. It gives a less noisy gradient estimate and fewer updates per epoch, and the gradient noise in small batches is part of why SGD generalises. Scaling batch size usually requires scaling the learning rate with it, and past some size the returns stop

What this looks like at scale
  • Residual connections are the reason modern depth works. ResNet's contribution was not depth but a gradient path that multiplies by 1. Every transformer block is a residual block for the same reason, and it is the clearest example of an architectural change made specifically to fix the product-of-factors problem above
  • Gradient checkpointing is standard for large models. Since backprop's memory cost is the stored activations, dropping most of them and recomputing during the backward pass trades roughly 30% extra compute for a large memory saving. It is what allows a model to train on hardware that cannot hold its activation stack
  • Mixed precision needs loss scaling, and the reason is this topic. fp16 has a narrow dynamic range, so small gradients underflow to zero. Multiplying the loss by a large constant before the backward pass shifts every gradient into representable range, and the optimiser divides it back out. Automatic loss scaling adjusts that constant when it detects overflow
  • Gradient-norm monitoring is routine instrumentation. Large training runs log per-layer gradient norms continuously, because a norm spike is the earliest warning of divergence — earlier than the loss, which is averaged and lags. Runs are checkpointed so a divergence can be rewound and resumed past the offending batch
  • Distributed training is an exercise in gradient plumbing. Data-parallel training computes gradients per device and all-reduces them; the communication of that gradient, not the arithmetic, is usually the bottleneck, which drives gradient compression, bucketing, and overlapping the reduction with the backward pass
🎯 In an interview

Explain backprop as reuse, not as calculus. "Finite differences cost two forward passes per parameter — for a billion parameters that is two billion passes per step. Backprop gets all of them in one backward pass, because the derivative with respect to an early weight contains the derivative with respect to every later one. Compute late, cache, reuse — it is dynamic programming on the chain rule." That answer explains why it matters, which the formula alone does not.

Expect: "What causes vanishing gradients?" — Give the product. The gradient into layer 1 is a product of one factor per layer, so a factor of 0.8 over 50 layers is 1.4 × 10⁻⁵ while 1.2 gives 9,100 — nine orders of magnitude from a ±20% deviation. Then make the point that lands: vanishing and exploding are the same instability, and initialisation, normalisation, residuals and ReLU are all attempts to hold that factor at 1. Residuals work because they give the gradient a path that multiplies by exactly 1.

Know the practical failure modes cold, because they are what the job consists of: zero_grad() because PyTorch accumulates; model.eval() because BatchNorm and dropout switch behaviour; log loss.item() or you leak the graph; clip by global norm. And that memory during training scales with activations, not parameters, which is why a model can infer but not train on the same card.

The differentiator: if BatchNorm comes up, note that "internal covariate shift" is the paper's explanation and is not well supported — the current understanding is that it smooths the loss landscape, since you can reintroduce covariate shift and keep the benefit. Very few candidates know the original justification did not survive scrutiny.
