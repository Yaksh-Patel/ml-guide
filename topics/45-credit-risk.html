Credit Risk & Scorecards
The one domain in this atlas where interpretability is a legal requirement rather than a preference, the probability must be honest because it gets multiplied by money, and the training data is systematically biased by the decisions your previous model made. Those three constraints — not the algorithms — are what make credit modelling its own discipline.
Expected loss, and why calibration is the whole job
Credit risk decomposes a loss into three estimable pieces, and the multiplication is what makes this domain different from ordinary classification:
$$\underbrace{\text{EL}}_{\text{expected loss}} = \underbrace{\text{PD}}_{\text{prob. of default}} \times \underbrace{\text{LGD}}_{\text{loss given default}} \times \underbrace{\text{EAD}}_{\text{exposure at default}}$$
A £20,000 exposure with a 3% PD and 45% LGD carries an expected loss of £270. Now suppose the model is well-ranked but poorly calibrated and the true PD is 6%:
Stated PDTrue PDProvisioned ELActual ELShortfall
3%6%£270£540100% under-provisioned
The ranking was perfect and the number was wrong, and only the number reaches the balance sheet. That is why calibration is not a refinement here — a model with excellent Gini and miscalibrated PDs will price correctly relative to itself and provision incorrectly in absolute terms, and the error scales with the size of the book. Every limit, price and provision is built on the absolute PD, which is the precise reason this domain distrusts models that only rank well.
It is also why resampling and class weighting are treated with suspicion in regulated models. Both deliberately change the base rate the model believes in, so both destroy calibration by construction. If you use them, recalibrate to the true observed default rate before the output goes anywhere near a provision.
Weight of evidence, computed
WoE replaces a bin with the log-odds of default within it, relative to the portfolio. The point is that it converts anything — ordinal, nominal, missing — into a single monotonic numeric scale that a logistic regression can use and a regulator can read.
$$\text{WoE}_i = \ln\!\left(\frac{\text{bad}_i / \text{total bad}}{\text{good}_i / \text{total good}}\right), \qquad \text{IV} = \sum_i \big(\text{bad\%}_i - \text{good\%}_i\big)\cdot \text{WoE}_i$$

Read three things off that table. Negative WoE means safer than the portfolio average and positive means riskier, so the sign carries meaning and the coefficient on a WoE feature should come out positive. IV = 0.254 is a strong single predictor — the conventional bands are below 0.02 useless, 0.1–0.3 strong, and above 0.5 suspicious, because a single variable that separates that well is usually a proxy for the outcome rather than a cause of it.
And the third, which people misread: monotonicity is required in the bad rate, not in the bad count share. Above, bins 1 and 4 have the same 0.240 share of bads while their default rates are 3% and 12% — the rates rise monotonically, which is what makes the scorecard defensible. Binning is chosen to enforce that monotonic rate, and a bin that breaks it is merged with a neighbour, because a scorecard where more utilisation sometimes means less risk cannot survive review no matter what it does for Gini.
Collections & Behavioral Scoring
Unlike application scoring (predict if new customer will default), behavioral scoring updates monthly using payment history — far more predictive.
  • Features: DPD (days past due) history, utilization rate, payment-to-minimum ratio, number of missed EMIs, balance trends
  • Triggers: predict which accounts to call proactively (early intervention)
  • Segmentation: early delinquency (1–30 DPD) vs late (90+ DPD) — different propensity models, different treatment strategies
  • Roll-rate analysis: what % of 30-DPD accounts roll to 60-DPD? Key input to provisioning models
Reject inference: your training data is a biased sample
This is the structural problem unique to lending and the one most often missing from a candidate's answer. You only observe repayment behaviour for applicants you approved. The ones you declined have no outcome — not a missing label, but an outcome that was never allowed to happen.
So the training sample is exactly the population your previous policy selected as good risks, and the model learns the relationship between features and default within the accept region only. Two consequences follow:
  • The model cannot know what it does not approve. If the old cutoff declined everyone below a score, the data contains no evidence about them, and the new model's behaviour there is extrapolation dressed as prediction
  • Measured performance describes the wrong population, and you cannot reason out which way it is wrong. Your Gini is computed on approved accounts while the model is applied to all applicants. Two effects pull against each other: the accepted range is narrower, which attenuates measured discrimination, while the accepted population is also easier to rank. Simulating it across several incumbent policies and seeds, the reported Gini came out anywhere from 0.09 below the through-the-door figure to 0.05 above it — the sign flips with the policy. What is systematic is the cost to the model itself: training on accepts only lost between 0.014 and 0.081 Gini against a model trained on the full through-the-door population, in every configuration tested

That last row is the honest one and the reason this problem persists: the only clean fix costs money by design, so it has to be justified as the price of knowing where your cutoff should be. Lenders that run one treat it as a permanent research budget rather than a one-off study.
The related timing constraint: the performance window must be long enough for defaults to mature. With a 90-days-past-due definition and a 12-month window, an account opened 4 months ago cannot yet be labelled — so recent, most-relevant data is unusable, and every scorecard is trained on a world at least a year old. That lag is not a modelling flaw, it is the shape of the problem, and it is why population stability monitoring matters as much as it does.
Monitoring: PSI computed, and why it leads performance
Gini tells you whether the model still discriminates. It can only tell you that after outcomes mature — which, per the window above, is a year late. PSI tells you whether the population has moved, and it is available immediately.
$$\text{PSI} = \sum_i \big(\text{actual\%}_i - \text{expected\%}_i\big)\cdot\ln\!\left(\frac{\text{actual\%}_i}{\text{expected\%}_i}\right)$$

Note the shape of the formula: it is symmetrised relative entropy, so it penalises movement in either direction and grows quickly once a band's share halves or doubles. The conventional bands — under 0.10 stable, 0.10–0.25 investigate, above 0.25 rebuild — are convention rather than theory, but they are the convention a validator will apply.
The reason this matters more than it looks: PSI is a leading indicator and Gini is a lagging one. A shift in the applicant mix shows up in PSI this month and in default performance next year. Teams that monitor only discrimination discover the problem three quarters after it started, which is also three quarters of mispriced lending. Track CSI per feature alongside it — a stable overall score can hide two features drifting in offsetting directions, and knowing which input moved is what makes the fix tractable.

IFRS 9 & Basel Modeling
  • IFRS 9 staging: Stage 1 (no significant deterioration) → Stage 2 (significant increase in credit risk) → Stage 3 (default). Models predict stage transitions.
  • Lifetime PD (IFRS 9): probability of defaulting at any point over the remaining life of the loan. Requires scenario-conditional forecasting (macro overlays).
  • Point-in-time (PIT) vs Through-the-cycle (TTC): IFRS 9 requires PIT (current conditions); Basel requires TTC (long-run average). Different calibration targets.
  • Macro overlays: adjust base PD for GDP growth, unemployment rate, interest rate scenarios
Mistakes that survive code review
  • Observation point and performance window not cleanly separated. Features must be strictly as-at the observation date and the label strictly from the window after it. Any overlap is leakage, and in credit it is the most common one — often via a field that gets updated when an account deteriorates
  • Ignoring reject inference entirely. Your sample is the population your last policy approved. The model has no evidence about the region you are proposing to lend into
  • Quoting AUC as the headline. Not wrong, but the domain speaks Gini (2·AUC − 1) and KS. AUC 0.75 is Gini 0.50; the application-scorecard floor of Gini 0.30 is AUC 0.65. Using the local vocabulary signals domain familiarity
  • Trusting a well-ranked, uncalibrated PD. It goes into a multiplication. Ranking is enough to sort a queue and only calibration survives being multiplied by exposure
  • Resampling or class weighting without recalibrating. Both change the implied base rate on purpose. Fine for ranking, disqualifying for provisioning
  • Confusing point-in-time with through-the-cycle. IFRS 9 wants PIT — current conditions reflected. Basel IRB wants TTC — a long-run average. A PIT model used for capital under-states requirements in good times and over-states them in bad, which is procyclical in exactly the way the rules exist to prevent
  • Unconstrained GBMs. Without monotone constraints a tree will happily learn that a band of higher income is riskier because of noise in one segment. It may improve Gini and it will not pass review. Constrain the direction of every variable where the direction is a policy commitment
  • Monitoring performance but not population. Gini lags by the length of your outcome window. PSI and CSI are available now
  • Non-monotonic bins. Merge them. A scorecard where more utilisation sometimes means less risk is indefensible regardless of its statistics
  • Using a feature you cannot justify to a regulator. Legality is not the only test — proxies for protected characteristics fail on disparate impact even when the attribute itself is absent, and postcode is the classic case
How It's Used at Top Companies
  • FICO (credit bureau scoring): logistic regression with WOE features remains the industry standard for bureau scores — regulatory auditability, interpretable scorecards, adverse action notice generation using top-4 SHAP features per applicant
  • Upstart (alternative credit scoring): XGBoost with 1000+ non-traditional variables (education, employment history, cash flow patterns) for thin-file applicants; CFPB-supervised model validation ensures no disparate impact across protected groups
  • Stripe (Radar — merchant risk): ML scorecard for merchant fraud risk at onboarding; combines graph features (network connections to known bad merchants), velocity features, and business verification signals; threshold tuned to balance fraud loss vs false friction on legitimate merchants
  • HSBC / Barclays (IFRS 9 provisioning): PD × LGD × EAD models for each loan segment; macro-conditional scenario models (base/adverse/optimistic) weighted to compute expected credit losses; quarterly model validation by independent risk function
🎯 In an interview

Open on the three constraints, because they explain every technique. "Interpretability is mandated rather than preferred; the probability is multiplied by exposure so it must be calibrated and not merely well-ranked; and the training data is biased by the previous policy's decisions. WoE plus logistic regression, monotone constraints, and reject inference are all consequences of those three."

Be able to compute WoE and IV. WoE is the log of (bin's share of bads / bin's share of goods), so negative is safer than average and positive is riskier; IV sums (bad% − good%)·WoE across bins, and 0.1–0.3 is a strong predictor while above 0.5 usually means a proxy for the outcome. Then the detail that shows real experience: monotonicity is required in the bad rate, not the bad count share — two bins can hold the same share of defaults at very different default rates.

Raise reject inference unprompted. You only observe outcomes for approved applicants, so the model learns the accept region and extrapolates beyond it, and your Gini is measured on a population that is not the one you apply the model to. Name the remedies honestly: bureau outcome data is the best obtainable information, inference techniques propagate an assumption rather than adding information, and the only clean fix is deliberately approving a small random sample below the cutoff — which costs real losses by design.

On monitoring, make the leading/lagging distinction. With a 90+ DPD definition and a 12-month window, Gini cannot tell you anything for a year — so PSI is the early warning. Have the arithmetic: bands moving from 25/25/25/25 to 10/20/30/40 gives PSI 0.228, in the investigate-now range. And track CSI per feature, because a stable overall score can hide two features drifting in offsetting directions. Speak Gini and KS, not AUC.
