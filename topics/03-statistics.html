Statistics & Inference
How to say something about a population when all you have is a sample — and how to know when you are not entitled to say it. Every experiment result, every "the model improved", every confidence number in a report is an inference claim, and a surprising share of them are made slightly wrong.
The p-value, stated correctly once
A p-value is the probability of seeing data at least this extreme, assuming the null hypothesis is true. That is the whole definition, and every common misuse comes from dropping the second half of it.
$$p = \mathbb{P}(\text{data at least this extreme} \mid H_0 \text{ true})$$
⚠️ three things it is not

Not the probability the null is true. That would be P(H₀ | data) — the conditional the other way round, and getting it requires a prior.

Not the probability your result was luck.

Not a measure of effect size. With a large enough sample, a completely irrelevant difference reaches p < 0.001. Report the effect and its interval alongside it, always.

the coin intuition

Flip a coin 10 times, get 9 heads. Under "the coin is fair", how often would you see something that lopsided? About 2% of the time. That 2% is the p-value. It does not say there is a 98% chance the coin is loaded — a trick coin was unlikely before you started, and the p-value knows nothing about that.

Type I error (α) — crying wolf
You reject the null when it was true. You shipped a change that does nothing, and reported a win.
🔥 fire alarm, no fire

α is the rate you choose. Setting α = 0.05 is a decision to be wrong this way 5% of the time when there is no effect. It is a budget, not a property of the world.

Test twenty independent things at α = 0.05 and you expect one false positive by construction. That is not bad luck; it is the definition. Correct for it — Bonferroni is crude but honest, Benjamini–Hochberg is better once you have many tests.
Type II error (β) — missing it
You fail to reject when there really was an effect. The change worked and you threw it away.
🏠 real fire, no alarm

Power = 1 − β is the probability of detecting a real effect of a given size. 80% is the usual target, which means you accept a 20% chance of missing a real improvement — worth saying out loud when someone asks why the test was inconclusive.

Power depends on sample size, effect size, variance and α. Underpowered tests are the most common experimental failure in industry, and in the write-up they look identical to "no effect".
Confidence intervals, and why they beat p-values
An interval tells you the size of the thing you found, not merely that you found something. "The lift is 2.3% ± 0.4%" answers the business question. "p = 0.03" does not.
$$\bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \qquad\qquad \text{SE} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$
💡 what the "95%" attaches to

It is a property of the procedure, not of your one interval. Repeat the whole experiment many times and 95% of the intervals you construct would contain the true value. "There is a 95% probability the true mean is in this interval" is the Bayesian statement, and it needs a prior to be true.

The √n in the denominator is the expensive part. Halving an interval takes four times the data. That is why "collect more data" stops being a strategy quickly, and why variance reduction — stratification, CUPED, paired designs — is where experienced experimenters actually spend their effort.
Central limit theorem, and why √n is expensive
When the formula runs out: bootstrap
Parametric intervals need a sampling distribution you can write down. For a median, a 90th percentile, a Gini coefficient or a ratio of two metrics, often you cannot. The bootstrap skips the algebra entirely.
  • Resample your data with replacement, same size, a few thousand times
  • Recompute the statistic on every resample
  • Take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of those values
It works because the empirical distribution stands in for the population. It fails when the statistic depends on the extreme tail (a maximum), when the sample is tiny, and when observations are dependent — time series need a block bootstrap, not a plain one.

Mistakes that invalidate real experiments
  • Peeking. Checking significance daily and stopping when it crosses 0.05 pushes the false-positive rate well past 5% — often past 20%. Either fix the sample size in advance, or use a sequential test built for continuous monitoring
  • p-hacking by segment. Slicing by country, device and cohort until something is significant is the twenty-tests problem in disguise. Decide the segments before you look
  • Assuming equal variances. Student's t-test assumes them; Welch's does not, and costs nothing. Use Welch by default — in scipy it is one argument
  • Confusing statistical and practical significance. At n = 10 million a 0.01% lift is significant and irrelevant
  • Reading non-significance as proof of no effect. "We did not detect it" is not "it is not there", especially at 80% power. An interval that spans zero and spans a large effect means inconclusive, not negative
  • Ignoring multiple comparisons across metrics. Ten guardrail metrics at α = 0.05 means one is expected to trip on a completely neutral change
How this is actually run at scale
  • Fixed-horizon or sequential, decided up front. Mature experimentation platforms enforce this in tooling precisely because peeking is otherwise irresistible
  • Variance reduction beats more traffic. CUPED uses pre-experiment data as a covariate and routinely cuts variance 30–50% — equivalent to a large traffic increase, for free
  • Guardrails beside the target metric. A checkout change that lifts conversion and raises p99 latency is not a win. Guardrails are how you notice — and they need multiple-comparison discipline of their own
  • Model comparison is an experiment too. "New model has better AUC" needs an interval. Bootstrap the metric over the test set, or compare paired predictions; a single point estimate on one split is not evidence
🎯 In an interview

Define the p-value precisely, unprompted. "The probability of data this extreme or more, given the null is true." Then say what it is not. Interviewers ask this specifically because so many candidates state the conditional backwards.

Expect: "The A/B test has run a week and just hit p = 0.04. Do you ship?" — The right answer starts with a question: was the sample size fixed in advance? If not, this is peeking and the number is not trustworthy. That reply is the thing being tested.

Also common: "How would you get a confidence interval for a median?" — Bootstrap. Naming it immediately signals practical experience.

Strong close: when asked how to make a test more sensitive, most candidates say "more data". Say CUPED or stratification, and explain that √n makes traffic an expensive lever.

In code
A confidence interval and a bootstrap interval for the same statistic. When the sampling distribution is unknown or the statistic is awkward (a median, a Gini), the bootstrap just works.
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.lognormal(mean=1.0, sigma=0.8, size=300)   # skewed, like most real data

# Parametric CI for the mean — relies on the CLT, fine at n=300
se = x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(x))
lo, hi = stats.t.interval(0.95, len(x) - 1, loc=x.mean(), scale=se)
print(f"mean 95% CI      [{lo:.2f}, {hi:.2f}]")

# Bootstrap CI for the MEDIAN — no formula needed
boot = np.array([np.median(rng.choice(x, len(x), replace=True)) for _ in range(5000)])
print(f"median 95% CI    [{np.percentile(boot, 2.5):.2f}, {np.percentile(boot, 97.5):.2f}]")

# Two-sample test: Welch by default, never assume equal variances
y = rng.lognormal(1.15, 0.8, 280)
t, p = stats.ttest_ind(x, y, equal_var=False)
print(f"Welch t={t:.2f}  p={p:.4f}")
