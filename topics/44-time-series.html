Time Series Modeling
Every other topic here assumes rows are exchangeable. Here they are not, and almost every mistake in the field is that assumption leaking back in — a shuffled split, a centred rolling window, a feature computed over the whole series. The models are the easy part; the discipline is making sure nothing in your pipeline knows something it could not have known at the time.
The baseline you have to beat, and the metric that tells you
Forecasting has an unusually strong trivial baseline, and skipping it is how teams ship models that are worse than nothing. Seasonal naive predicts that this period equals the same period one season ago — this Tuesday looks like last Tuesday, this December like last December. It has no parameters and it is genuinely hard to beat.
So the honest scoreboard is a ratio against it. MASE — mean absolute scaled error — divides your error by the naive forecast's error on the same data:
$$\text{MASE} = \frac{\text{MAE}(\text{model})}{\text{MAE}(\text{seasonal naive})}$$
MASEMeaning
0.820% better than naive — a real result
1.0you have reproduced the baseline with more machinery
1.2worse than doing nothing, and you cannot tell from MAE alone
Report MASE, or report MAE alongside the naive MAE. A bare MAE of 8 is uninterpretable — it is excellent if naive scores 20 and embarrassing if naive scores 6. This is the single cheapest safeguard in forecasting and it is routinely skipped, which is why the M-competitions kept finding that simple methods beat elaborate ones.
MAPE is the most-used and most-broken forecast metric

It has two failures that change decisions. It explodes near zero: actual 1, forecast 2 scores 100%, while actual 100, forecast 101 scores 1% — the same absolute error, a hundredfold difference in penalty. So any series with small or zero values is dominated by its quietest periods, and MAPE is undefined outright at zero.

And it is asymmetric in a specific direction. Under-forecasting is bounded: forecast 0 against an actual of 100 scores 100% and you cannot do worse. Over-forecasting is unbounded: forecast 300 against 100 scores 200%. So minimising MAPE systematically biases you toward under-forecasting — which for inventory means stockouts, for staffing means understaffing, and for capacity means outages. If you optimise MAPE you have chosen that bias without deciding to. Use MAE, MASE, or a quantile loss where the asymmetry is set deliberately.

Forward-chaining validation, and the rolling-window leak
Leakage, which here is arithmetic rather than judgement
Temporal leakage is easier to create than in any other setting, because most convenient library defaults are wrong for ordered data. Four specific mechanisms:
MechanismWhat happens
Shuffled splitThe model trains on next month and is tested on last month. Scores look superb and mean nothing. Any train_test_split without shuffle=False does this
Centred rolling windowA 7-day mean at time t computed from t−3 to t+3 contains three days of future. Pandas' rolling() is trailing by default, which is correct — but center=True and any .interpolate() across a gap are not
Whole-series statisticsScaling by the series mean and standard deviation, or target-encoding a category on the full history, puts the future into every row. The scaler must be fitted on the training window only
Revision and reporting lagThe subtlest one. GDP, ad spend and CRM fields are revised after the fact, so today's database value for last month is not the value that existed last month. Training on revised data and serving on unrevised data is a leak no split can catch
That last row is the one that survives careful reviewers, and the fix is a discipline rather than a code change: ask for every feature what its value was as at the prediction time, not what it is now. Systems that get this right store data with both an event time and a knowledge time, which is exactly what a feature store's point-in-time correctness guarantees.
forward-chaining, and what it costs you

The correct validation is forward-chaining: train on [0, t], validate on (t, t+h], step forward, repeat. Every fold trains only on its own past, and averaging the folds gives an honest estimate — with two consequences worth stating.

First, k folds means k model fits, so it is k times the cost of a single split. Second, and more subtly, every fold trains on less data than your final model will — the earliest fold may have a fraction of the history — so forward-chaining CV tends to underestimate the deployed model's accuracy. That is the right direction to be wrong in, and it is worth saying out loud when someone asks why the live model beats its backtest.

Stationarity: what it is for, and how to stop
A series is stationary when its mean, variance and autocorrelation do not depend on when you look. It matters because classical methods estimate one set of parameters for the whole series — if the mean is drifting, there is no single mean to estimate, and the model is fitting a moving target.
The Augmented Dickey–Fuller test has a null hypothesis of non-stationarity, which is the direction people get backwards: p < 0.05 lets you reject non-stationarity, so a small p-value is the good outcome. A large p-value is not proof of a unit root, merely failure to rule one out — which is why KPSS, whose null is the opposite, is often run alongside it.
Differencing is the usual remedy and the usual overreach:
  • d = 1 removes a linear trend. Almost always enough for business series
  • d = 2 removes a changing trend, and is where you should get suspicious
  • Over-differencing injects noise. Differencing amplifies high-frequency variation, so an unnecessary difference makes the series harder to forecast while passing the stationarity test. The symptom is a large negative spike at lag 1 in the ACF of the differenced series
  • Prefer a log or Box–Cox transform for changing variance, and differencing only for changing level. They are different problems and differencing does not fix a variance that grows with the level
Tree-based models do not need stationarity — and they have the opposite problem. A tree predicts a leaf mean, so it cannot extrapolate past the range it was trained on: a model trained on a growing series will under-forecast every future period, structurally. The fix is the same transformation for a different reason — model differences or ratios rather than levels, so the target stays inside the training range even as the level rises.

Features, and the horizon you forgot to check
Handing an ordered problem to a gradient-boosted model works well and is often the strongest baseline after seasonal naive. The features are formulaic:
  • Lags at the periods that matter — t−1, t−7, t−28 for daily data. These are what let a tabular model see autocorrelation that ARIMA models explicitly
  • Rolling statistics over trailing windows — mean, standard deviation, min/max over the past 7, 30, 90 days. The standard deviation is often more predictive than the mean, because volatility regime-shifts before level does
  • Calendar features, cyclically encoded so hour 23 sits next to hour 0 rather than 23 units away
  • Known-future covariates — holidays, promotions, scheduled events. These are legitimately available at prediction time and are frequently the largest single lift
a lag of 1 is only available at horizon 1

The mistake that survives every leakage review: building features with a lag-1 term and then forecasting 7 days ahead. At the moment you predict day t+7, you do not know day t+6 — so lag_1 does not exist for that prediction. Backtests pass because the historical frame has every column filled in; production silently receives a missing or stale value.

Every feature must be available at the horizon you actually forecast. For a 7-day horizon your shortest usable lag is 7. The alternatives are recursive forecasting (predict t+1, feed it back in, and accept compounding error) or training a separate model per horizon — which is what most production systems do, because it is honest about what is knowable.

Mistakes that survive code review
  • Any shuffled split. The defining error. shuffle=False, or TimeSeriesSplit
  • No naive baseline. Without it, a MAE is an uninterpretable number and you cannot tell whether the project succeeded
  • Optimising MAPE. Explodes near zero and biases toward under-forecasting, so it converts a metric choice into a stockout policy
  • Features unavailable at the forecast horizon. A lag-1 feature in a 7-day-ahead model. Backtests pass, production breaks
  • Scaling or encoding on the full series. Fit inside the training window, exactly as for any other model
  • Ignoring data revisions. If a source restates history, your training data never existed at prediction time. Ask what each value was as at the prediction date
  • Trees on a trending target. They cannot extrapolate; they will under-forecast growth forever. Difference the target
  • Reading a large ADF p-value as proof of a unit root. Failure to reject is not evidence for the null. Pair it with KPSS
  • Over-differencing. Passes the test, injects noise, and shows up as a big negative lag-1 autocorrelation
  • Treating calendar artefacts as signal. Weekend gaps, month-end batch postings, fiscal boundaries and reporting delays produce patterns that are properties of your data pipeline, not the world. Plot the series before modelling it

🎯 In an interview

Lead with the baseline, not the model. "First thing I fit is seasonal naive, and I report MASE — my MAE divided by the naive MAE — because a bare MAE is uninterpretable. MASE above 1 means the model is worse than assuming this Tuesday looks like last Tuesday." That answer immediately signals you have done this rather than read about it, and it is the reason the M-competitions keep finding simple methods win.

Be specific about leakage mechanisms. Shuffled splits, centred rolling windows, whole-series scaling — and then the one that separates candidates: data revisions. Sources restate history, so today's stored value for last month is not what existed last month, and no train/test split can catch it. The question to ask of every feature is what its value was as at the prediction time.

Expect: "How would you validate a forecasting model?" — Forward-chaining: train on [0, t], validate on (t, t+h], roll forward. Then volunteer the cost and the bias: k folds is k fits, and every fold trains on less data than the final model will, so the estimate is conservative. Being able to say which direction your validation errs in is a strong signal.

The feature-engineering trap worth raising unprompted: every feature must exist at the horizon you forecast. A lag-1 term in a 7-day-ahead model is unavailable at prediction time — so either train one model per horizon, or forecast recursively and accept the compounding error. And note that MAPE's asymmetry is a business decision in disguise: it caps under-forecasting at 100% and leaves over-forecasting unbounded, so optimising it chooses stockouts.

In code
The three things that make time series different: you must not shuffle, you must test for stationarity, and your baseline is harder to beat than you think.
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller
from sklearn.model_selection import TimeSeriesSplit

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 730
t = pd.date_range('2024-01-01', periods=n, freq='D')
y = pd.Series(50 + 0.05*np.arange(n) + 8*np.sin(2*np.pi*np.arange(n)/365)
              + rng.normal(0, 2, n), index=t)

# 1. STATIONARITY -- ARIMA and friends assume it. Test, then difference.
print("level  ADF p =", round(adfuller(y)[1], 4))          # >0.05: has a unit root
print("diff   ADF p =", round(adfuller(y.diff().dropna())[1], 6))

# 2. FEATURES must only look backwards. shift() before rolling, every time.
df = pd.DataFrame({'y': y})
df['lag_1']   = df.y.shift(1)
df['lag_7']   = df.y.shift(7)
df['roll_28'] = df.y.shift(1).rolling(28).mean()           # shift FIRST
df['dow']     = df.index.dayofweek
# df.y.rolling(28).mean() would include today -> leakage, and a beautiful CV score

# 3. VALIDATION is forward-chaining. Never KFold.
for i, (tr, va) in enumerate(TimeSeriesSplit(4).split(df)):
    print(f"  fold {i}: train {len(tr):4d} -> validate {len(va)}  "
          f"({df.index[tr[-1]].date()} | {df.index[va[0]].date()})")

# 4. BASELINE: seasonal naive. Publishing a model that loses to this is common.
print(f"seasonal naive MAE = {(df.y - df.y.shift(7)).abs().mean():.3f}"
      "  -- beat this before claiming anything")
