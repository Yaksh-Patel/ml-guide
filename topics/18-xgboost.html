XGBoost — Deep Dive
Gradient boosting says "fit a tree to the gradient" and leaves open how the tree is grown and what its leaf values should be. XGBoost answers both by writing regularisation into the objective and solving it exactly — so the leaf value, the split score, and the pruning rule all fall out of one equation instead of being three separate heuristics. That derivation is the interview question, and it is short enough to do from memory.
The objective, with complexity priced in
Standard boosting minimises training loss and controls complexity from outside — via depth caps and a learning rate. XGBoost puts the cost of complexity inside the thing being minimised:
$$\mathcal{L} = \sum_i L\big(y_i, \hat y_i\big) \;+\; \sum_k \Omega(f_k), \qquad \Omega(f) = \underbrace{\gamma T}_{\text{price per leaf}} + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda\|w\|^2}_{\text{L2 on leaf values}} + \underbrace{\alpha\|w\|_1}_{\text{L1 on leaf values}}$$
where T is the number of leaves in the tree and w the vector of leaf output values. Two things are being charged for: having leaves at all (γ) and leaves with large outputs (λ, α). Everything below is a consequence of asking what tree minimises this.
Second order, and why it buys a closed form
Expand the loss at round t around the current predictions to second order. Writing gi for the first derivative and hi for the second, both evaluated at the previous round's prediction:
$$\mathcal{L}^{(t)} \simeq \sum_i \Big[ g_i f_t(x_i) + \tfrac{1}{2} h_i f_t(x_i)^2 \Big] + \gamma T + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \sum_j w_j^2$$
Now fix the tree's structure and ask for its best leaf values. Every row in leaf j gets the same output wj, so grouping the sums by leaf and writing Gj = Σgi, Hj = Σhi over the rows landing in that leaf, each leaf contributes Gjwj + ½(Hj + λ)wj². That is a one-dimensional quadratic in wj — so it has an exact minimum, not an approximate one:
$$w_j^{*} = -\frac{G_j}{H_j + \lambda} \qquad\text{with optimal value}\qquad -\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{G_j^2}{H_j + \lambda}$$
This is what the Hessian buys. First-order boosting knows only which direction to move and must borrow a step size from the learning rate; second-order boosting knows the curvature, so it can compute how far to move in one shot. Where the loss is flat (small h) it takes a big step; where the loss curves sharply (large h) it takes a small one. That is also why λ appears in a denominator — it is ridge regression on the leaf values:
Leaf with G = −6, H = 3w* = 6/(3 + λ)
λ = 02.00 — unregularised
λ = 11.50
λ = 100.46 — leaf outputs pulled hard toward zero
Small leaves are the ones λ affects most: a leaf with few rows has a small H, so λ dominates its denominator and its output is shrunk aggressively. Regularisation is automatically strongest exactly where the evidence is thinnest, which is a much better behaviour than a uniform cap on depth.
The split score is not a heuristic — it is the objective
Since every structure has a known optimal value, comparing two structures is arithmetic. Splitting one leaf into L and R replaces one −½G²/(H+λ) term with two, and adds one leaf at price γ:
$$\text{Gain} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda} - \frac{G^2}{H+\lambda}\right] - \gamma$$
Compare this with the Gini gain of an ordinary decision tree: that one measures class purity, a proxy for what you care about. This measures the actual reduction in the actual objective. Nothing is being approximated except the second-order Taylor step itself.
Work one through. A node has G = 4, H = 10, and λ = 1. A candidate split sends gradient mass 6 with Hessian 5 to the left and −2 with Hessian 5 to the right:
$$\begin{aligned} \text{Gain} &= \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\frac{6^2}{5+1} + \frac{(-2)^2}{5+1} - \frac{4^2}{10+1}\right] - \gamma \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2}\big[6.000 + 0.667 - 1.455\big] - \gamma \;=\; \mathbf{2.606} - \gamma \end{aligned}$$

0+2.606split accepted

3−0.394rejected — this is the pruning
So γ is a minimum-gain threshold denominated in the units of the objective, and pruning is not a separate pass over a grown tree — a split that does not pay γ is never made. Note also why this split was worth making: the two children have gradients of opposite sign (leaf values −1.00 and +0.33), meaning the rows were pulling in different directions and averaging them was costing real objective. That is what a good split looks like in gradient space.
XGBoost = gradient boosting + four engineering decisions
min_child_weight is a Hessian sum, and that surprises people
It reads like min_samples_leaf and it is not. The constraint is on ΣH in the leaf, and what the Hessian means depends entirely on the loss.
$$\text{squared loss: } h_i = 1 \qquad\qquad \text{log loss: } h_i = p_i(1-p_i)$$
For regression with squared loss, h = 1 for every row, so ΣH is exactly the row count and min_child_weight does behave like a minimum sample size. For classification it does not, because p(1 − p) collapses as predictions become confident:

A hundred rows can fail a threshold of 1. That is the intended behaviour and it is rather elegant: the parameter counts useful rows, not rows. A hundred already-well-predicted examples carry almost no information about how to improve, so refusing to build a leaf for them is correct. But it means the number has no intuitive scale on a classification problem — min_child_weight=1 is a far weaker constraint than min_samples_leaf=1 sounds, and raising it to 10 or 50 is a normal, strong regularisation move rather than an extreme one.
Missing values, learned rather than imputed
At every candidate split, XGBoost puts the non-missing rows on their sides, then tries the missing rows both ways and keeps whichever gives more gain. That direction is stored in the node.
This is genuinely better than imputation, because missingness is frequently informative — an unfilled income field is not a typical income, it is a different kind of applicant. Mean-imputing throws that signal away; XGBoost gets to use it. It is also fast, since the sparsity-aware pass only visits present values.
the production bug this creates

The direction is learned from the missing values seen in training. If a column has no missing values in your training data and starts arriving as NaN in production — a broken upstream join, a new client who does not send the field — then no direction was ever learned for it and the rows go left by default. The model does not error and the predictions are quietly meaningless for those rows. Check that your training data's missingness pattern matches production's, and if a field can go missing later, make sure some of it is missing now.

The engineering that made it feasible
  • Histogram / approximate splits. Exact greedy search considers every distinct value as a threshold. Bucket each feature into ~256 bins and the search is over 256 candidates instead. On 10 million rows that is roughly 40,000× fewer candidate evaluations per feature per node, at a cost in split precision that is almost never measurable. This is the single change that took boosting from research to production scale, and LightGBM pushed it further
  • Column blocks, sorted once. Feature values are pre-sorted into compressed blocks and reused at every node, so the O(n log n) sort is paid once per training run rather than once per split
  • Cache-aware prefetching. Gradient statistics are gathered into contiguous buffers, because the naive access pattern is a random walk through memory and cache misses dominate the runtime
  • Out-of-core blocks. Compressed, shardable blocks let it train on data larger than RAM — which mattered a great deal in 2016 and still does on a single large machine
  • Level-wise growth. Expanding one whole depth at a time is implicitly conservative: every leaf gets the same budget. LightGBM's leaf-wise alternative always splits the highest-gain leaf anywhere, which fits faster and overfits more readily
Monotone constraints: the feature that passed model risk review
A boosted ensemble is free to learn that default risk falls then rises with income, because some noisy region of the training data said so. That is indefensible in a regulated setting even when it improves validation AUC.
Setting monotone_constraints makes XGBoost reject any split that would violate the required direction, and clamp child leaf values so the ordering holds across the whole tree. The guarantee is structural, not statistical — it holds for every possible input, not merely on the data you tested. You can hand a validator a proof rather than a chart.
This, plus TreeSHAP for per-applicant adverse-action reasons, is the whole story of how a non-linear model became acceptable in credit decisioning where logistic scorecards were effectively mandatory. Expect to give up a little accuracy; the constraint is a real restriction. Expect that to be an acceptable trade, because a model that cannot be deployed has an accuracy of zero.
Mistakes that survive code review
  • Imputing before XGBoost. You are replacing information with a guess. Pass the NaNs through and let the split learn a direction — then verify that production missingness looks like training missingness
  • Reading min_child_weight as a row count. It is a Hessian sum. On classification, 1 is very permissive; on regression it is literally a row count. Two different meanings, one parameter name
  • Leaving γ at 0 and controlling complexity only with depth. γ is the one knob that prunes on the objective itself, which is strictly better-targeted than a blanket depth cap. It is underused
  • Assuming max_depth transfers to LightGBM. Leaf-wise growth means depth is the wrong handle — num_leaves and min_data_in_leaf are the controls, and a LightGBM model with num_leaves=255 is far more complex than an XGBoost model at depth 8 despite looking comparable
  • Forgetting the seed with subsampling on. Two runs then differ, and you cannot distinguish a real gain from a reseed
  • Skipping scale_pos_weight on imbalanced data — or setting it and then quoting the probabilities. It multiplies positive gradients, so it deliberately shifts the implied base rate
  • Reaching for XGBoost on 500 rows. Its whole advantage is capacity plus regularisation at scale. Below a few thousand rows, a regularised linear model or an SVM usually generalises better and always explains itself better
  • Using it where extrapolation is required. The leaves are constants, so the ensemble is a staircase capped by the training range — the tree limitation, inherited whole
Where it runs, and what replaced it where
  • The default first model for tabular problems, still. Mixed types, missing values, non-linear interactions, hundreds of columns: it trains in minutes on a CPU and is very hard to beat. This is why it remains the baseline that any deep tabular architecture is measured against
  • Credit and insurance risk. Monotone constraints plus SHAP is the combination that satisfies validation and adverse-action requirements. Retraining is scheduled, drift is monitored, and constraint sets are documented artefacts reviewed alongside the model
  • Fraud and abuse. Fast enough to retrain daily on a rolling window, which matters because adversaries adapt. Time-based splitting is non-negotiable here — the failure mode is a leak, not a bad fit
  • Ranking and CTR. Pairwise and listwise ranking objectives are supported directly, since boosting only needs a gradient. Inference is a few hundred short traversals, so single-digit millisecond serving is routine
  • Displaced by LightGBM above roughly half a million rows, where leaf-wise growth and GOSS give most of the accuracy at a fraction of the training time; and by CatBoost when raw high-cardinality categoricals dominate and its ordered target statistics prevent an encoding leak you would otherwise have to hand-build
🎯 In an interview

"How does XGBoost differ from vanilla gradient boosting?" is the question, and the answer is the derivation. Take the loss to second order, and each leaf's contribution becomes a quadratic Gw + ½(H+λ)w². Minimise it: w* = −G/(H+λ), worth −½G²/(H+λ). Sum over leaves and the split gain is just the difference between structures, minus γ for the extra leaf. Leaf value, split score and pruning rule all come from one equation — that is the contribution, and being able to walk it through is the differentiator.

Expect: "Why does the second-order term help?" — Curvature gives a step size. First-order boosting knows the direction and takes whatever step the learning rate dictates; with the Hessian, the optimal leaf value is available in closed form — large steps where the loss is flat, small where it curves.

Expect: "How does it handle missing values?" — It tries both directions at each split and keeps the higher-gain one, storing that direction in the node. Then add the failure case, because it shows production experience: if nothing was missing in training, no direction was learned, and NaNs arriving later go left silently.

Worth volunteering: that min_child_weight is a Hessian sum, so on classification a hundred confident rows can sum to less than 1 — it counts informative rows rather than rows. And that monotone constraints are why boosting is deployable in regulated credit at all. Both are things people who have only tuned XGBoost do not say.

In code
The three things XGBoost adds over plain gradient boosting — second-order gradients, structural regularisation, and early stopping on a real validation set.
import numpy as np
import xgboost as xgb
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

X, y = make_classification(20_000, n_features=30, weights=[.95], random_state=0)
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=.25, stratify=y, random_state=0)

model = xgb.XGBClassifier(
    n_estimators=2000,          # deliberately too many; early stopping picks the point
    learning_rate=0.05,
    max_depth=5,
    min_child_weight=5,         # min sum of hessian in a leaf, not a row count
    subsample=0.8, colsample_bytree=0.8,
    reg_lambda=1.0, reg_alpha=0.0,
    gamma=0.0,                  # min loss reduction to make a split
    scale_pos_weight=(ytr == 0).sum() / (ytr == 1).sum(),   # imbalance, not resampling
    eval_metric='aucpr',        # PR-AUC for a 5% positive rate
    early_stopping_rounds=50,
    tree_method='hist',
)
model.fit(Xtr, ytr, eval_set=[(Xte, yte)], verbose=False)

print("best iteration", model.best_iteration, "of 2000")
print("test PR-AUC   ", roc_auc_score(yte, model.predict_proba(Xte)[:, 1]).round(4))

# Monotone constraints: the feature that makes XGBoost defensible in credit risk
mono = xgb.XGBClassifier(n_estimators=200, monotone_constraints='(1,' + '0,'*28 + '0)')
