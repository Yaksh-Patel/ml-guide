Evaluation Metrics
The metric is not a reporting detail chosen at the end — it is the objective, and picking it decides which model wins. Most arguments about whether a model is good are really unstated disagreements about which error is more expensive.
Classification: what each number is blind to
$$\begin{aligned} \text{precision} &= \frac{TP}{TP + FP} \quad \text{— of the ones I flagged, how many were real} \\ \text{recall} &= \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \quad \text{— of the real ones, how many did I catch} \\ F_1 &= 2 \cdot \frac{\text{precision} \cdot \text{recall}}{\text{precision} + \text{recall}} \end{aligned}$$
Precision and recall answer different questions and move in opposite directions as you slide the threshold. F₁ is their harmonic mean, which punishes imbalance between them — a model at 0.99 precision and 0.01 recall scores F₁ ≈ 0.02, where the arithmetic mean would have flattered it at 0.5.
accuracy is the trap, with numbers

Fraud at a 3% base rate. Predict "not fraud" for every transaction: 97% accuracy, zero fraud caught, no model required. Accuracy is only meaningful when classes are roughly balanced and the two error types cost about the same — which is almost never true for anything worth modelling.

F₁ has its own blind spot: it ignores true negatives entirely and weights precision and recall equally, which is a business assumption disguised as a default. If a false negative costs ten times a false positive, use F₂ or state the cost ratio explicitly.
Threshold-free: ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC
Both summarise a model across every threshold at once, which makes them useful for comparing models before you have chosen an operating point. They disagree sharply under imbalance, and knowing why is the whole skill.

worked — why they diverge

10,000 transactions, 100 fraudulent. A model flags 300 to catch 90. Recall 0.90, precision 0.30. The 210 false positives against 9,900 negatives is an FPR of 2.1% — so ROC-AUC looks excellent. But an analyst reviewing those 300 alerts finds 7 in 10 are wasted work. PR-AUC reflects the analyst's experience; ROC-AUC does not.

One model, one ranking, a different trade at every threshold
Regression: the choice is about outliers
$$\begin{aligned} \text{MAE} &= \tfrac{1}{n}\sum |y - \hat{y}| \\ \text{RMSE} &= \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n}\sum (y - \hat{y})^2} \\ R^2 &= 1 - \frac{\sum (y-\hat{y})^2}{\sum (y-\bar{y})^2} \end{aligned}$$
  • MAE treats every error proportionally. Optimising it predicts the conditional median
  • RMSE squares first, so one large miss dominates. Optimising it predicts the conditional mean
  • MAPE is scale-free and explodes near zero — never use it on a target that can be small
  • R² is the fraction of variance explained, and can go negative when the model is worse than predicting the mean
Errors of 1, 1, 1, 10: MAE = 3.25, RMSE = 5.15. The gap between the two is your outlier problem, quantified. If they diverge, decide deliberately whether those outliers are the thing you care about or noise you want to ignore.
Ranking: position matters
For search, feeds and recommendations, being right about item 1 is worth more than being right about item 40. Pointwise metrics cannot express that.
  • Precision@k / Recall@k — the simplest honest framing: users only see the first k
  • MAP — averages precision at each relevant hit, so earlier hits count more
  • NDCG — discounts by log(position) and supports graded relevance. The standard for search
  • MRR — one over the rank of the first correct answer. Right when there is a single target
A model with excellent AUC can rank badly at the top of the list, because AUC averages over all pairs and most pairs are deep in the tail where nobody looks.

Ask what happens to a prediction after it leaves the model. Something acts on it — a queue, a limit, a page of results — and that action defines the metric. Choosing the metric before understanding the action is how teams optimise the wrong thing competently for months.
Mistakes that make a metric misleading
  • Quoting accuracy on imbalanced data. The single most common one. Always give the base rate next to any classification number
  • Comparing PR-AUC across datasets. Its baseline is the positive rate, so 0.4 on a 3% base rate is strong and 0.4 on a 40% base rate is poor. ROC-AUC is comparable across datasets; PR-AUC is not
  • Optimising a threshold metric during training, then shipping the default 0.5. Threshold choice is a separate decision made on business cost, not a constant inherited from a library
  • Averaging metrics over imbalanced classes without saying how. Macro treats every class equally, micro weights by frequency, weighted sits between. They can differ by tens of points and the word "F1" hides which one you used
  • Reporting a point estimate from one split. Bootstrap the metric or report fold spread. A 0.01 AUC difference on one test set is usually noise
  • Ignoring calibration because AUC is good. Ranking and probability are different properties. If a downstream calculation multiplies your score by an amount of money, AUC does not tell you whether the score is honest
  • Letting the offline metric drift from the business metric. AUC up, revenue flat is a normal outcome. Any offline metric is a proxy, and the proxy needs periodic re-validation against reality
How this is handled at scale
  • Alert capacity sets the metric. If the fraud team can review 500 cases a day, the metric is precision@500 — not AUC, not F₁. Fixed-capacity review is extremely common and almost never reflected in the metric people report
  • Cost-weighted decisions, not thresholds. Mature systems compute expected value per action — P(fraud) × amount versus review cost — and act on that. The threshold falls out of the arithmetic instead of being tuned
  • Guardrails alongside the target. A ranking change that lifts clicks and cuts long-term retention is a loss. The target metric alone will never tell you
  • Segment-level reporting. One aggregate number hides a model that works on the majority group and fails on a minority one. Reporting by segment is both a fairness requirement and a good way to find broken features
🎯 In an interview

Never answer "which metric" without asking what the prediction is used for. Handed "we're detecting fraud", ask how many alerts can be reviewed per day, and what a missed fraud costs versus a false alarm. Then name the metric. The question is testing whether you connect metrics to decisions.

Expect: "Why is accuracy a bad metric for fraud?" — Give the 3% base rate and the 97% do-nothing model. Concrete numbers end that question immediately.

Then: "ROC-AUC or PR-AUC?" — PR-AUC when positives are rare, because FPR's denominator is enormous so ROC barely moves while precision collapses. Add that PR-AUC's baseline is the positive rate, so it is not comparable across datasets.

Strong close: distinguish ranking from calibration and say when each matters. It is the fastest way to show you have shipped a model into a decision system.

In code
Pick the metric before you fit anything. This prints the whole panel at once so the trade-off is visible rather than argued about.
import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import (accuracy_score, precision_recall_fscore_support,
                             roc_auc_score, average_precision_score, brier_score_loss)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
y = (rng.random(10_000) < 0.03).astype(int)          # 3% positives
p = np.clip(rng.beta(2, 8, 10_000) + 0.5 * y, 0, 1)  # scores, mildly informative

# Accuracy is useless here: predicting all-zero already scores 0.97
print("all-zero accuracy", accuracy_score(y, np.zeros_like(y)).round(4))

for thr in (0.5, 0.3, 0.15):
    pr, rc, f1, _ = precision_recall_fscore_support(y, (p > thr).astype(int),
                                                    average='binary', zero_division=0)
    print(f"thr={thr:<5} precision={pr:.3f} recall={rc:.3f} f1={f1:.3f}")

# Threshold-free: ROC-AUC flatters imbalance, PR-AUC does not
print("roc_auc ", roc_auc_score(y, p).round(4))
print("pr_auc  ", average_precision_score(y, p).round(4))   # baseline = prevalence
print("brier   ", brier_score_loss(y, p).round(4))          # calibration, not ranking
