Cross-Validation
Every model-selection decision you make consumes a little of your test set's credibility. Cross-validation is how you make those decisions without spending the one honest estimate you have left — and how you find out that your excellent score was leakage, before someone else does.
k-fold, and what the number it gives you actually is
Split the data into k parts. Train on k−1, validate on the one left out, rotate, and average. Every row is used for validation exactly once and for training k−1 times.
$$\text{CV}_k = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} L\big(f^{(-i)}, \mathcal{D}_i\big)$$
Two numbers come out, and the second one matters as much as the first. The mean estimates performance; the standard deviation across folds estimates how much you should trust that estimate. A CV result of 0.83 ± 0.01 and one of 0.83 ± 0.09 are completely different situations, and reporting only the mean hides which one you have.
choosing k

k = 5 or 10 is the default and the reason is bias–variance, again. Small k means each model trains on less data, so the estimate is pessimistically biased. Large k means the k training sets overlap almost entirely, so the fold scores are highly correlated and the averaging buys you less than it appears to. Leave-one-out (k = n) is nearly unbiased and has notoriously high variance — plus it costs n fits.

The mistake that inflates every score you report
Any transformation fitted on the whole dataset before splitting has already seen the validation rows. The scaler's mean, the imputer's median, the encoder's category means, the feature selector's chosen columns — all of them leak.
$$\underbrace{\text{fit scaler on all } X}_{\text{leak}} \;\rightarrow\; \text{split} \qquad\text{vs}\qquad \text{split} \;\rightarrow\; \underbrace{\text{fit scaler on train fold only}}_{\text{correct}}$$
The damage from a scaler is usually small. From target encoding or feature selection it can be enormous — a model that scores 0.95 in CV and 0.71 in production, with no bug anywhere except the order of two lines.
the habit that removes the whole class of bug

Put every transformation inside a Pipeline and pass the pipeline to cross_val_score. sklearn then refits each step on the training fold of every split, automatically. This is not a style preference — it is the difference between a trustworthy number and a flattering one, and it costs one line.

The grouped case is the one most often missed. If your dataset has 50,000 rows from 3,000 users, plain KFold puts the same user on both sides of the split. The model learns "user 1,417 tends to convert" — which is real signal in CV and useless for a new user. Ask "what is the unit I will actually predict for?" and group by that.

Nested CV: the honest number after tuning
Once you have used CV to pick hyperparameters, that CV score is no longer an unbiased estimate of anything — you selected the configuration that happened to look best on those folds. The optimism is real and often several points.
$$\underbrace{\text{outer loop}}_{\text{estimates performance}} \;\supset\; \underbrace{\text{inner loop}}_{\text{selects hyperparameters}}$$
Nested CV separates the two jobs: the inner loop tunes, the outer loop scores a model whose hyperparameters were chosen without ever seeing the outer validation fold. It costs (outer × inner) fits, which is why people skip it — but you should at least know the number you are quoting is optimistic if you do.
the cheap version

Hold out a genuine test set at the very start, tune with plain CV on the remainder, and touch the test set exactly once at the end. It is not as statistically clean as nested CV, it costs one extra fit instead of dozens, and it prevents the same failure. The discipline that matters is not looking.

Mistakes that make CV lie to you
  • Preprocessing before the split. The headline case above. If you cannot point at a Pipeline, assume you are leaking
  • Shuffling time series. KFold on ordered data trains on the future. The score will be excellent and the model will fail on day one
  • Ignoring groups. Same patient, user or session on both sides of the split. Extremely common in medical and clickstream data
  • Oversampling before splitting. SMOTE on the full dataset synthesises validation rows from training rows and vice versa. It must go inside the pipeline, and imblearn's pipeline exists precisely for this
  • Reporting the mean without the spread. Two models at 0.83 are indistinguishable if the fold standard deviation is 0.04. Picking the higher mean is then noise-chasing, not model selection
  • Selecting features on the whole dataset. "I picked the top 50 features by correlation with the target, then cross-validated" — the selection already used every label. This one produces the most spectacular leakage
  • Tuning until CV improves. Enough attempts and you will overfit the validation folds themselves. Track how many configurations you have tried; it is a real budget
How this is handled in production settings
  • Time-based splits are the norm, not the exception. Almost any model that scores live traffic has a temporal structure, so validation is forward-chaining: train on everything up to a cutoff, validate after it, roll the cutoff forward. The number this gives is lower than random KFold and it is the number that comes true
  • A frozen holdback that no one touches. Separate from validation, often a recent time window, opened only to sanity-check a candidate before release. Its value is entirely in how rarely it is used
  • CV variance drives retraining cadence. If fold-to-fold spread is large relative to the improvement you are claiming, you cannot detect whether a retrain helped — which is an argument for scheduled retraining rather than metric-triggered
  • Group-aware splits are a data-contract question. Whether your unit of prediction is a row, a session or a customer determines the splitter, and getting it wrong is invisible in every offline metric
🎯 In an interview

Lead with the leak. Asked to describe cross-validation, describe it in two lines and then spend your answer on what invalidates it: preprocessing outside the fold, temporal order, and groups. That ordering shows you have debugged a suspiciously good score rather than only read the definition.

Expect: "Your CV score is 0.95 and production is 0.71. What happened?" — Work through it out loud: leakage from preprocessing or feature selection, a group appearing on both sides, temporal shuffling, or genuine distribution shift since training. Naming all four and saying which you would check first is the answer.

Also common: "Why not leave-one-out?" — Nearly unbiased, high variance, and n fits. k = 5 or 10 is the practical trade.

Strong close: mention nested CV and that a tuned CV score is optimistic. Most candidates never separate selection from estimation.

In code
The three splitters that matter, and the one mistake that inflates every score you report: fitting the scaler before the split.
import numpy as np
from sklearn.model_selection import (KFold, StratifiedKFold, TimeSeriesSplit,
                                     cross_val_score)
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(500, 8)); y = (X[:, 0] + rng.normal(size=500) > 0).astype(int)

# WRONG — the scaler sees the validation fold, so the score is optimistic
X_scaled = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)
print("leaky   ", cross_val_score(LogisticRegression(), X_scaled, y, cv=5).mean().round(4))

# RIGHT — the pipeline refits the scaler inside every fold
pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression())
print("clean   ", cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=5).mean().round(4))

# Which splitter: stratify for imbalance, never shuffle time
print("stratified", cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True,
                                                                  random_state=0)).mean().round(4))
print("temporal  ", cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=TimeSeriesSplit(5)).mean().round(4))
