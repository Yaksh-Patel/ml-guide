Confusion Matrix & ROC
Four numbers, and every classification metric is a ratio of two of them. Learn to derive the metrics from the matrix rather than memorising them and the whole family stops being confusable — including which axis ROC uses and why AUC flatters an imbalanced problem.

$$\begin{aligned} \text{recall} = \text{TPR} = \text{sensitivity} &= \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \quad \text{(row of actual positives)} \\ \text{precision} &= \frac{TP}{TP + FP} \quad \text{(column of predicted positives)} \\ \text{FPR} &= \frac{FP}{FP + TN} \quad \text{(row of actual negatives)} \\ \text{specificity} = 1 - \text{FPR} &= \frac{TN}{TN + FP} \end{aligned}$$
the one thing worth memorising

Recall divides along the actual-positive row. Precision divides along the predicted-positive column. Same numerator, different denominator. Every other metric in this topic is built from those two directions, and getting the direction right is the whole game.

ROC: the matrix recomputed at every threshold
A classifier outputs scores, not classes. Pick a threshold and you get one confusion matrix and one (FPR, TPR) point. Sweep the threshold from 1 to 0 and you trace the ROC curve. The curve is not a different object from the matrix — it is the matrix, several hundred times.
$$\text{AUC} = \mathbb{P}\big(\hat{s}(x^{+}) > \hat{s}(x^{-})\big)$$
That identity is the interpretation worth carrying: AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative. It is a pure ranking measure — it says nothing about whether the scores are honest probabilities, and it is unchanged by any monotonic rescaling of them.
  • 0.5 — no better than a coin. The diagonal
  • Below 0.5 — your labels or signs are flipped. Invert and you have a working model
  • Above ~0.95 on real tabular data — suspect leakage before celebrating
Why AUC flatters rare-positive problems
FPR's denominator is every actual negative. When negatives outnumber positives 1,000 to 1, that denominator is enormous, so a large absolute number of false positives barely moves the x-axis.
worked — the same model, two metrics

100,000 transactions, 100 fraudulent. The model flags 1,000 and catches 90.

TPR = 90/100 = 0.90. FPR = 910/99,900 = 0.009. On an ROC curve that is a point in the top-left corner — near-perfect, AUC likely above 0.97.

Precision = 90/1,000 = 0.09. The analyst working the queue sees nine real cases in every hundred alerts. Same model, and PR-AUC would report roughly 0.09 against a 0.001 baseline.

Neither number is wrong. ROC answers "how well does it rank?", PR answers "how much of my reviewer's day is wasted?" — and only one of those is the operational question.

KS statistic — the banking standard
$$\text{KS} = \max_{t} \big| F_{\text{bad}}(t) - F_{\text{good}}(t) \big|$$
The largest vertical gap between the cumulative score distributions of the two classes. It answers a specific operational question: at which cutoff does this model separate best, and how much separation is there at that point.
  • KS below 20 — weak, generally not deployable for credit decisioning
  • 30 to 50 — the normal range for a working scorecard
  • Above 70 — investigate for leakage rather than celebrate
KS relates to AUC (Gini = 2·AUC − 1 is the other number quoted in the same rooms) but has the practical advantage of naming a threshold, which is what a policy team needs.
Multi-class: the matrix grows, the averaging becomes a choice
With C classes the matrix is C × C, and collapsing it to one number requires deciding how to weight classes. The three options can differ by tens of points.
  • Macro — average the per-class metric equally. A rare class counts as much as a common one. Use when every class matters equally
  • Micro — pool all TP/FP/FN, then compute once. Dominated by the frequent classes; equals accuracy for single-label problems
  • Weighted — average per-class, weighted by support. Sits between the two
Reporting "F1 = 0.82" without saying which average is a genuinely incomplete statement. Off-diagonal cells are also the most useful debugging artefact you have — they show exactly which pairs of classes the model conflates, which usually points at a labelling ambiguity rather than a model defect.
Mistakes that survive code review
  • Transposing the matrix. sklearn's confusion_matrix returns rows as actuals; many textbooks use the opposite. ravel() gives (tn, fp, fn, tp) in that order. Print it once with labels and confirm — precision and recall silently swap otherwise
  • Confusing FPR with 1 − precision. FPR divides by all actual negatives; 1 − precision divides by all predicted positives. Under imbalance these differ by orders of magnitude, and it is the mistake behind most "but the AUC was great" post-mortems
  • Reporting AUC on an imbalanced problem and stopping there. Report PR-AUC alongside, plus precision at the operating point you will actually use
  • Shipping the 0.5 threshold. 0.5 is a library default, not a decision. It is only right if the two errors cost the same and the model is calibrated
  • Believing AUC says anything about probability quality. Apply any monotonic squashing to your scores and AUC is identical while Brier score changes completely
  • Comparing AUCs from different test sets. Difficulty varies. Compare on the same data, and bootstrap the difference before believing a 0.005 gap
  • Ignoring FN cost asymmetry. In screening, a missed case can cost a thousand times a false alarm. No single-number metric encodes that unless you put it there
How this is used in practice
  • Credit and risk teams speak in KS and Gini. Not because AUC is wrong, but because KS names a cutoff and Gini has decades of institutional benchmarks behind it. Knowing the translation (Gini = 2·AUC − 1) is table stakes in those interviews
  • Fraud operates at fixed review capacity. The number that matters is precision at the top-k the team can actually work through, and the threshold is set by queue length rather than by a metric optimum
  • Medical screening picks the point on the curve deliberately. Regulators and clinicians choose an operating point by sensitivity requirement, then live with the resulting specificity. The curve is the artefact under discussion, not the AUC
  • Production monitoring watches the score distribution, not just the metric. Labels arrive late, so a shift in the predicted-score histogram is often your first warning — long before any confusion matrix can be computed
🎯 In an interview

Derive, do not recite. Asked for precision and recall, draw the four cells and say "recall divides along the actual-positive row, precision along the predicted-positive column". Deriving them takes ten seconds and is impossible to get backwards.

Expect: "Your fraud model has 0.97 AUC. Are you happy?" — Not yet. Ask the base rate, then walk the 100,000-transaction arithmetic: TPR 0.90, FPR 0.009, precision 0.09. Doing that out loud is the strongest possible answer.

Also common: "What does AUC actually mean?" — The probability a random positive outranks a random negative. Then note it is invariant to monotonic transformations, which is why it says nothing about calibration.

Do not say that FPR is one minus precision.

In code
Building the matrix by hand once fixes the axis confusion for good, then sweeping the threshold shows why one number can never describe a classifier.
import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import confusion_matrix, roc_curve, auc

y = np.array([0,0,0,0,1,1,1,0,1,0])
p = np.array([.1,.2,.6,.3,.9,.45,.8,.05,.55,.35])

tn, fp, fn, tp = confusion_matrix(y, (p >= .5).astype(int)).ravel()
print(f"TP={tp} FP={fp} FN={fn} TN={tn}")
print(f"recall/TPR = TP/(TP+FN) = {tp/(tp+fn):.2f}")
print(f"precision  = TP/(TP+FP) = {tp/(tp+fp):.2f}")
print(f"FPR        = FP/(FP+TN) = {fp/(fp+tn):.2f}")   # ROC's x-axis

# The ROC curve is just this matrix recomputed at every threshold
fpr, tpr, thr = roc_curve(y, p)
for f, t, th in zip(fpr, tpr, thr):
    print(f"thr={th:>5.2f}  FPR={f:.2f}  TPR={t:.2f}")
print("AUC =", round(auc(fpr, tpr), 3))
