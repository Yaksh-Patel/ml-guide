Calibration & Explainability
Two questions a good AUC cannot answer: does 0.8 actually mean 80%, and why did this particular row get that score. The first matters the moment a probability is multiplied by money. The second matters the moment a human has to defend the decision.
Calibration: is the number honest
A model is calibrated if, among all rows it scores 0.30, roughly 30% actually turn out positive. That is a completely separate property from ranking — a model can order every case perfectly and still be systematically wrong about probability.
$$\mathbb{P}\big(y = 1 \mid \hat{p} = p\big) = p \quad \text{for all } p$$
To see that these are independent: take a perfectly calibrated model and square all its scores. Every ranking is unchanged, so AUC is identical — and the model now understates every probability. Any monotonic transformation does this.
when it stops being academic

Expected loss = P(default) × exposure. If the model says 0.05 and the truth is 0.12, you have under-reserved by a factor of two and every limit, price and provision built on it is wrong. Ranking is enough to sort a queue; only calibration survives being multiplied.

  • Logistic regression is calibrated more or less by construction — it optimises log loss, which is a proper scoring rule
  • Tree ensembles are not. Random forests average votes and pull away from 0 and 1; boosted trees optimise a margin and push toward them. Both need fixing
  • Neural nets with modern training are usually overconfident — high capacity plus long training drives scores toward the extremes
  • Any resampling or class weighting destroys calibration by design: you deliberately changed the base rate the model believes in
Measuring and fixing it
Plot observed frequency against predicted probability in bins — the reliability curve. Below the diagonal means overconfident; above means underconfident. Then score it with a metric that rewards honesty rather than order.
$$\text{Brier} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (\hat{p}_i - y_i)^2 \qquad \text{log loss} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \big[y_i \log \hat{p}_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{p}_i)\big]$$
Both are proper scoring rules: they are minimised only by the true probability, so they cannot be gamed by shifting scores around. Log loss punishes confident mistakes far harder — one prediction of 0.001 on a positive case dominates the average, which is a feature when confidence is what you are grading.

SHAP: attribution with a guarantee
SHAP borrows a result from cooperative game theory. Treat each feature as a player and the prediction as the payout; a feature's SHAP value is its average marginal contribution across every possible ordering of the features.
$$\hat{f}(x) = \phi_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{M} \phi_j$$
That equation is the property that matters and the reason SHAP won: the attributions sum exactly to the prediction. Start at the base rate φ₀, add each feature's contribution, arrive at the model's output. Nothing is unexplained and nothing is double-counted — which is precisely what you need when someone asks why this application was declined.
  • Local: one row's φ values are an adverse-action explanation you can put in a letter
  • Global: mean |φ| per feature gives an importance ranking that, unlike impurity importance, is not biased by cardinality
  • TreeSHAP is exact and fast for tree ensembles. KernelSHAP is model-agnostic, approximate, and slow enough to matter
the limit to state out loud

SHAP explains the model, not the world. A large φ for income means the model leans on income — not that income causes default. Under correlated features the split between two collinear variables is somewhat arbitrary, and reading SHAP as causal is the most common misuse in production.

LIME, and why SHAP mostly replaced it
Perturb the row, see how predictions move, fit a small interpretable model to that local neighbourhood, and read its coefficients.
  • Genuinely model-agnostic and intuitive to explain to a non-specialist
  • Unstable: re-run it and the explanation can change, because the perturbation sample is random
  • No additivity guarantee — the pieces need not sum to the prediction
  • Neighbourhood definition is a free parameter that materially changes the answer
Still useful for images and text where the perturbation is natural (mask a region, drop a word). For tabular models, TreeSHAP is exact and reproducible, which regulated settings require.

Mistakes that reach production
  • Assuming a good AUC implies usable probabilities. They are different properties. Check the reliability curve before any expected-value calculation
  • Calibrating on training predictions. The calibrator learns to correct scores the model has memorised and generalises nothing
  • Resampling and then trusting the probabilities. SMOTE or class weighting shifts the implied base rate. If you need calibrated output, either skip resampling or recalibrate afterwards on the true prevalence
  • Reading SHAP as causal. "Reducing utilisation would lower this customer's risk" does not follow from a SHAP value. It is a statement about the model's mechanics
  • Quoting impurity importance. Cardinality bias makes it actively misleading; the classic demonstration is a random id ranking above real features
  • Explaining a model that is wrong. A clean SHAP plot on a leaking model explains the leak beautifully. Explanations validate reasoning, not correctness
  • Averaging SHAP without absolute value. Positive and negative contributions cancel, and an important feature looks unimportant. Global importance uses mean |φ|
Where this is mandatory rather than nice
  • Credit decisioning is legally required to explain. US adverse-action notices under ECOA/Reg B need the principal reasons for a decline, per applicant. SHAP's additivity is why it became the standard mechanism — the reasons provably account for the score
  • Any expected-value pipeline needs calibration as a build step. Pricing, limit setting, reserving, ad bidding: all multiply a probability by an amount, so calibration is validated and monitored alongside discrimination, not instead of it
  • Model risk review asks for both. A validation team will want discrimination (AUC/KS/Gini), calibration (reliability plus Brier), and stability (PSI) — and will ask what happens to each under a stressed population
  • Monitoring watches calibration drift separately. A model can keep its ranking power while its probabilities go stale, which happens whenever the base rate moves. Ranking metrics will not catch it
🎯 In an interview

Separate the two properties immediately. "AUC measures ranking; calibration measures whether the probability is honest. They are independent — square every score and AUC is unchanged while calibration breaks." That one sentence answers most calibration questions before they are fully asked.

Expect: "Your random forest has great AUC but the business says the probabilities are wrong." — Diagnose with a reliability curve, fix with isotonic or Platt on held-out data, and check whether anyone resampled the training set. Mention that forests are systematically under-confident at the extremes because they average votes.

On SHAP: lead with additivity — contributions sum to the prediction — then state the limit unprompted: it explains the model, not causality. Volunteering the limitation is what distinguishes a practitioner from someone who has read the README.

Do not quote feature_importances_ as evidence without noting its cardinality bias.

In code
A model can rank perfectly and still lie about probability. Reliability curves diagnose it; isotonic or Platt scaling fixes it without touching the ranking.
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.calibration import CalibratedClassifierCV, calibration_curve
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score, brier_score_loss
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

X, y = make_classification(20_000, n_features=20, weights=[.9], random_state=0)
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=.3, random_state=0)

raw = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, random_state=0).fit(Xtr, ytr)
cal = CalibratedClassifierCV(raw, method='isotonic', cv='prefit').fit(Xtr, ytr)

for name, m in [('raw', raw), ('isotonic', cal)]:
    p = m.predict_proba(Xte)[:, 1]
    print(f"{name:9} auc={roc_auc_score(yte, p):.4f}  brier={brier_score_loss(yte, p):.4f}")
    # AUC barely moves (ranking preserved), Brier improves (probabilities honest)

prob_true, prob_pred = calibration_curve(yte, cal.predict_proba(Xte)[:, 1], n_bins=10)
for pp, pt in zip(prob_pred, prob_true):
    print(f"  predicted {pp:.2f} -> observed {pt:.2f}")
