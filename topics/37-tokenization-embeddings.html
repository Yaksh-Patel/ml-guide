Tokenization & Embeddings
Two conversions sit before every language model, and both leak. Tokenization chops text into a fixed vocabulary, which decides what the model can even see — it is why models are bad at arithmetic and why non-English costs more per word. Embeddings then place those tokens in a geometry where distance is supposed to mean similarity. Most retrieval failures are one of these two steps behaving exactly as designed and not as assumed.
Why subwords: the trade tokenization is resolving
Tokenization is a compromise between two failure modes, and seeing both makes the subword answer inevitable.
GranularityVocabulary"unhappiness"Failure mode
Characters~10011 tokensnothing is ever unknown, and sequences are so long the model spends its context on spelling
Words200K+1 tokenshort sequences, and every unseen word — typo, name, "unhappinesses" — becomes [UNK], an information hole
Subwords (BPE)30K–100K2–3 tokenscommon words are single tokens, rare words decompose, and nothing is ever out-of-vocabulary
BPE gets there greedily: start from characters, repeatedly find the most frequent adjacent pair in the corpus and merge it into a new token, stopping at the target vocabulary size. Frequent words get merged into single tokens because they occur often; rare words are left as assemblies of frequent pieces. The vocabulary is therefore a compression artefact of the training corpus, which is the root of everything in the next card.
The variants differ in the merge criterion rather than the idea. WordPiece (BERT) merges the pair that most increases corpus likelihood rather than the most frequent one. SentencePiece operates on raw bytes with no pre-tokenisation step, which is what makes it work for languages that do not delimit words with spaces. Byte-level BPE (GPT-2 onward) uses bytes as the base alphabet, so any possible input is representable and [UNK] stops existing entirely.
The consequences people meet in production
Because the vocabulary was fit to a corpus, anything unlike that corpus costs more tokens — and tokens are simultaneously the context limit, the latency and the bill.
The rule of thumb worth memorising: for English, roughly 4 characters or 0.75 words per token. So 1,000 tokens is about 4,000 characters or 750 words. Now watch it break:
  • Non-English text costs 2–3× more tokens per word because the merges were learned mostly from English. The same document translated is a larger prompt, a longer latency and a bigger bill — and it consumes context window faster, so a model's advertised limit is effectively smaller in some languages than others. This is a fairness and cost issue, not a curiosity
  • Numbers tokenise inconsistently. Depending on the tokeniser, "1234" may become ["123","4"] or ["12","34"] while "1235" becomes something else entirely. The model therefore never sees a stable representation of a numeral's digits, which is a large part of why LLMs are unreliable at arithmetic. Newer tokenisers deliberately split digits individually to fix this
  • Whitespace is part of the token. In byte-level BPE, " the" and "the" are different tokens. A prompt that ends with a trailing space can therefore change the output, and a hand-built few-shot template with inconsistent spacing degrades quality for no visible reason
  • Character-level tasks are structurally hard. Asking a model to reverse a string or count letters is asking it to work below its own perceptual unit. It does not see "strawberry" as ten letters; it sees two or three opaque tokens
  • Morphological boundaries are not respected. BPE splits by frequency, not by meaning, so "unhappy" may be one token while "unhappiness" splits in a place no linguist would choose. For NER, dates and codes, inspect the tokenisation before assuming the model can see the structure
check the tokeniser, not the character count

"Our documents are under 512 characters so BERT is fine" is a category error — 512 characters is not 512 tokens, and specialised text runs well above the English average. Financial, medical and legal jargon, identifiers, and code all tokenise densely.

Run your actual corpus through the actual tokeniser and look at the length distribution, not the mean. Silent truncation at the model's limit removes the end of every long document, and it shows up later as a mysterious accuracy ceiling on exactly the cases that matter most.

Static embeddings, and the analogy result that is oversold
Word2Vec and GloVe assign one fixed vector per word, learned from co-occurrence. The remarkable finding was that the geometry ends up carrying relational structure nobody put there:
$$\text{vec}(\text{king}) - \text{vec}(\text{man}) + \text{vec}(\text{woman}) \approx \text{vec}(\text{queen})$$
the caveat that is almost always omitted

That result only works because the search for the nearest vector excludes the three input words. Leave them in and the nearest neighbour of the computed point is usually "king" itself — the arithmetic moves you a short distance from where you started, and "king" is still closer than "queen".

The relational structure is real and weaker than the demonstration implies. Worth knowing because it is a clean example of a famous result whose framing does some of the work, and because the same caution applies to reading structure off any embedding space.

The fatal limitation is polysemy. One vector per word means "bank" gets a single point that must serve river banks and savings banks at once — so it lands somewhere between, useful for neither. Contextual models fix this by construction: the representation is a function of the whole sentence, so "bank" in "river bank" and "bank" in "savings bank" are simply different vectors. That is the entire reason static embeddings were superseded.
FastText is worth remembering as the useful middle: it represents a word as the sum of its character n-grams, so it can embed a word it has never seen and it handles morphologically rich languages far better. Still static, but not helpless in the face of a typo.
Embedding Space Properties
$$\begin{aligned} king - man + woman \approx queen \quad \text{[Word2Vec analogy]} \\ cosine_similarity(Paris, France) \approx cosine_similarity(Berlin, Germany) \\ Nearest neighbors of "cat" \approx_{dog,kitten,feline,pet} \end{aligned}$$
Embeddings encode semantic relationships. Arithmetic in embedding space reflects semantic relationships — the famous "king - man + woman = queen" property. This emerges purely from co-occurrence statistics, not explicit semantic rules.
Sentence embeddings: why [CLS] disappoints
A very common mistake is to take BERT's [CLS] vector as a sentence embedding and find it performs badly at similarity. It is not a bug — the token was never trained for that job.
[CLS] was supervised by next-sentence prediction, a nearly trivial binary task, so nothing ever pushed it to be a faithful summary of meaning. Mean pooling over token states beats raw [CLS] consistently, and a model actually trained on a similarity objective beats both:
ApproachUse when
Averaged static word vectorsa baseline. Surprisingly hard to beat on short texts, and free
Raw [CLS] from a pretrained encoderessentially never — it was trained on NSP, not similarity
Mean pooling of token states (mask out padding)a reasonable off-the-shelf sentence vector with no training
Sentence-BERT / bi-encodersthe right answer for semantic search — trained with a contrastive objective so cosine distance means what you want
Cross-encoder (both texts in one forward pass)reranking the top-k. Much more accurate and cannot be pre-indexed, since it scores pairs
The bi-encoder versus cross-encoder split is the architecture of every production search system. A bi-encoder embeds documents offline so retrieval is one ANN lookup; a cross-encoder must run the model on every candidate pair, which is impossible over millions of documents and excellent over the fifty a bi-encoder returned. Retrieve wide and cheap, rerank narrow and expensive.
Storage is a real design constraint. One million documents at 1,536 dimensions in fp32 is 6.1 GB of vectors before any index overhead. Matryoshka-trained embeddings are built so they can be truncated — the same vectors at 256 dimensions are 1.02 GB, a 6× saving for a modest quality cost, and you can store the short version for the first-pass search and the long one only where precision matters.
Mistakes that survive code review
  • Comparing embeddings from different models. Each model has its own arbitrary geometry, so cosine similarity between a vector from one encoder and one from another is meaningless. Query and corpus must be embedded by the same model — and re-embedding the whole corpus is the real cost of changing embedding models
  • Not L2-normalising before cosine similarity. Cosine is the dot product of unit vectors. Feed unnormalised vectors to an index configured for inner product and you have silently ranked by magnitude as well as direction — and magnitude often encodes document length
  • Using raw [CLS] for similarity. Trained on NSP; use mean pooling, or a model trained for the purpose
  • Assuming character counts predict token counts. They do not, and specialised text is worse. Tokenise your corpus and look at the distribution
  • Expecting reliable arithmetic or character manipulation. Digits tokenise inconsistently and the model cannot see letters. Use a tool call for arithmetic rather than tuning prompts
  • Ignoring trailing whitespace in prompts and few-shot templates. " the" and "the" are different tokens; inconsistent spacing measurably degrades output
  • Chunking documents without overlap for RAG. A hard split at a fixed token count will cut sentences and separate a claim from its qualifier. Overlap, and prefer splitting on structural boundaries over exact token counts
  • Treating cosine similarity as relevance. High similarity means the embedding model considered the texts alike, which frequently means topically alike rather than answer-bearing. This is why reranking exists
  • Adding tokens to a tokeniser without resizing the embedding matrix. The new ids index past the end of the table. This one does error, eventually, and usually in a confusing place
How It's Used at Top Companies
  • OpenAI (text-embedding-3): MRL-trained embeddings usable at any dimension from 256 to 3072; the standard embedding model for RAG and semantic search in the OpenAI ecosystem; internally fine-tuned on massive retrieval datasets
  • Google (Universal Sentence Encoder / Gecko): dual-encoder architecture trained on massive web data for semantic similarity; the embedding backbone behind Google's passage retrieval in Search and Bard/Gemini RAG
  • Spotify (podcast search): SBERT fine-tuned on podcast transcripts for semantic search; user query embedded at search time, episode embeddings pre-computed and indexed with FAISS — retrieval under 5ms at scale
  • Airbnb (listing search): dual-encoder model trained on (query, clicked listing) pairs; listing embeddings computed offline and indexed; query embedding computed at search time and used for ANN retrieval — production semantic search system serving millions of queries/day
🎯 In an interview

Explain subwords as the resolution of a trade-off. "Character-level never has unknown words but the sequences are so long the model spends its context on spelling; word-level is compact but every unseen word becomes [UNK]. BPE merges frequent adjacent pairs until it hits a vocabulary budget, so common words are single tokens, rare words decompose into frequent pieces, and nothing is out-of-vocabulary." That framing explains why the answer looks the way it does.

Then show you have hit the consequences. The vocabulary is fitted to a corpus, so non-English text costs 2–3× more tokens per word — a cost, latency and effective-context-window difference, not a curiosity. Digits tokenise inconsistently, which is a real part of why arithmetic is unreliable. And know the rule of thumb: about 4 characters or 0.75 words per token in English.

Expect: "How would you build semantic search?" — Bi-encoder to embed the corpus offline, ANN index, embed the query at request time, retrieve top-k, then rerank with a cross-encoder. The reason for two stages is the point: a cross-encoder scores pairs so it cannot be pre-indexed, but it is far more accurate over a shortlist. Mention L2-normalising before cosine, and that changing embedding model means re-embedding everything.

Two things that stand out. That raw [CLS] is a poor sentence embedding because it was supervised by next-sentence prediction rather than similarity — the reason, not just the fact. And if king − man + woman comes up, note that the analogy only resolves to "queen" when the input words are excluded from the nearest-neighbour search; leave them in and you get "king" back.

In code
BPE merges learned from a tiny corpus, so the algorithm is visible rather than described — then the three properties of embeddings that interviews actually test.
import re
from collections import Counter

# Byte-pair encoding: start from characters, repeatedly merge the most
# frequent adjacent pair. That is the entire algorithm.
corpus = {'low': 5, 'lower': 2, 'newest': 6, 'widest': 3}
vocab = {' '.join(w) + ' </w>': c for w, c in corpus.items()}

for step in range(4):
    pairs = Counter()
    for word, freq in vocab.items():
        sym = word.split()
        for a, b in zip(sym, sym[1:]):
            pairs[(a, b)] += freq
    if not pairs: break
    best = max(pairs, key=pairs.get)
    print(f"merge {step+1}: {best} (count {pairs[best]})")
    bigram = re.escape(' '.join(best))
    vocab = {re.sub(rf'(?<!\S){bigram}(?!\S)', ''.join(best), w): c
             for w, c in vocab.items()}
print(list(vocab)[:4])
