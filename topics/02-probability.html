Probability Theory
The part of maths that tells you how much to believe something. Every classifier that outputs a score is making a probability claim, and most of the ways those claims go wrong — miscalibration, base-rate neglect, the independence assumption in Naive Bayes — are failures of the handful of rules below.
The three rules everything else is built from
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(A \cup B) &= \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B) - \mathbb{P}(A \cap B) \\ \mathbb{P}(A \cap B) &= \mathbb{P}(A) \cdot \mathbb{P}(B \mid A) \\ \mathbb{P}(A \mid B) &= \frac{\mathbb{P}(A \cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(B)} \\ \text{independent:}\quad \mathbb{P}(A \cap B) &= \mathbb{P}(A) \cdot \mathbb{P}(B) \end{aligned}$$
The subtraction in the first line is there because the overlap would otherwise be counted twice. The second line is the important one: joint probability is not a product unless the events are independent, and in real data they essentially never are.
the intuition to keep

Conditioning is shrinking the world. P(A | B) asks: having thrown away every outcome where B did not happen, what fraction of what remains has A? That is why the denominator is P(B) — it renormalises to the smaller world.

Bayes' theorem, and why the prior dominates
$$\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(H \mid E)}_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{\overbrace{\mathbb{P}(E \mid H)}^{\text{likelihood}} \cdot \overbrace{\mathbb{P}(H)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(E)}_{\text{evidence}}}$$
Read it as an update rule: you had a belief, evidence arrived, here is the revised belief. The denominator is just the total probability of seeing that evidence at all, across every hypothesis — it is what makes the posterior sum to one.
The counter-intuitive part is that a very accurate test can still leave you at a coin flip, because accuracy is a property of the test and the answer depends on the population.
the trap, with the numbers

A disease affects 1% of people. A test is 99% sensitive and 99% specific. You test positive. Out of 10,000 people: 100 are sick and 99 of them test positive; 9,900 are healthy and 1% of those — 99 people — also test positive. So 198 positive tests, of which 99 are real.

P(sick | positive) = 99 / 198 = 50%. Nothing is wrong with the test. The false positives are simply drawn from a pool 99× larger.

Expectation and variance, and the identity that saves algebra
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[X] &= \sum_x x\,\mathbb{P}(x) \\ \operatorname{Var}(X) &= \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 \\ \mathbb{E}[aX + bY] &= a\mathbb{E}[X] + b\mathbb{E}[Y] \quad \text{(always)} \\ \operatorname{Var}(X + Y) &= \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y) \quad \text{(only if independent)} \end{aligned}$$
Expectation is linear unconditionally. Variance is not. That asymmetry is why averaging n independent models divides variance by n — the whole statistical argument for bagging — and why it stops working when the models are correlated.
worked

A fair die: E[X] = 3.5, E[X²] = 15.167, so Var = 15.167 − 12.25 = 2.917. Two dice summed: E = 7, and because they are independent, Var = 5.833. Correlate them and that second number is simply wrong.

Independence, conditional independence, and the difference that matters
These are not the same thing, and the gap between them is where Naive Bayes lives and where confounding hides.
  • Independent: knowing B tells you nothing about A. Rare in real data
  • Conditionally independent given C: once you know C, B tells you nothing more about A. Common, and much weaker
Ice-cream sales and drowning deaths are strongly correlated. Condition on temperature and the correlation largely disappears — they were never related, they were both driven by the weather. Temperature is a confounder, and this is the same structure that makes an unadjusted treatment effect come out with the wrong sign.
why Naive Bayes gets away with it

Naive Bayes assumes features are conditionally independent given the class. For text that is plainly false — "space" and "shuttle" co-occur constantly. The classifier still works, because a systematically over-confident product biases every class in the same direction, and the argmax survives. Use it for the ranking; never quote its probabilities.

Mistakes that show up in real models
  • Base-rate neglect. Quoting model precision without the prevalence it was measured at. A fraud model with 90% precision on a 5% base rate is a different object from one measured at 0.1%
  • Treating P(A|B) as P(B|A). "99% of fraudsters do X" is not "99% of people doing X are fraudsters". This inversion is the single most common probability error in production reasoning, and it is exactly what the rare-disease example above formalises
  • Assuming independence to make the algebra work. Multiplying probabilities of correlated events understates joint risk badly — this is roughly how correlated mortgage defaults were mispriced in 2008
  • Confusing a probability with a frequency. A model's 0.7 output is only "70 out of 100 such cases" if the model is calibrated. Most tree ensembles are not, out of the box
  • Forgetting the law of total probability. When P(E) is hard, expand it: P(E) = Σ P(E | Hᵢ)P(Hᵢ). Nearly every "impossible" Bayes question is this one line
Where this bites in production
  • Fraud and credit scoring. Both are Bayesian updates at heart: a prior risk from the population, a likelihood from behaviour, a posterior that drives a decision. Model the base rate wrong and every downstream threshold is wrong
  • Calibration for decisioning. If a score feeds an expected-value calculation — expected loss = P(default) × exposure — then the probability has to mean what it says. Ranking is not enough; this is why calibration is a separate step
  • Thompson sampling. Maintain a Beta posterior per arm, sample from each, play the winner. Exploration falls out of the posterior width instead of needing an ε schedule
  • A/B testing. A p-value is P(data this extreme | no effect), not P(no effect | data). Reporting it as the latter is the most common statistical misstatement in product reviews
🎯 In an interview

The rare-disease question will come up in some form. Do not reach for the formula — build the 10,000-person table out loud. It is faster, it is impossible to get backwards, and it shows the interviewer you understand why the answer is 50% rather than that you memorised Bayes.

Expect: "When does Var(X+Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)?" — only under independence (strictly, zero covariance). The follow-up is usually why bagging helps less than 1/n suggests: the trees are correlated, so the covariance term does not vanish.

Also common: "What is the difference between independence and conditional independence?" — Give the ice-cream and drowning example. It lands immediately and sets you up to talk about confounding.

Do not say that a p-value is the probability the null is true.
