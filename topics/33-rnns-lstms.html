RNNs & LSTMs
A recurrent network applies the same weights at every timestep — weight sharing across time, exactly as a convolution shares across space. That reuse is what lets one model handle any sequence length, and it is also what breaks it, because reusing one matrix a hundred times means raising it to the hundredth power. Nearly everything in this topic follows from that single consequence.
One matrix, raised to the length of your sequence
The recurrence is one line, and the problem is visible in it:
$$h_t = \tanh\big(W_{hh}h_{t-1} + W_{xh}x_t + b\big)$$
Because the same Whh applies at every step, the gradient from step t back to step 1 involves that one matrix multiplied by itself t times. Its behaviour is therefore governed by a single number — the largest singular value λ:
λ of WhhGradient factor over 100 stepsResult
0.92.7 × 10⁻⁵nothing more than ~20 steps back can be learned
1.01.0the only stable value — and it is a knife edge
1.113,781NaN, usually within a few batches
This is the product-of-factors instability that governs depth, but sharper. In a deep feed-forward network each layer has its own matrix and the errors partially cancel; here one matrix's spectral radius is raised to a power, so nothing averages out. A vanilla RNN is not "hard to train on long sequences" — it is arithmetically unable to carry gradient past a couple of dozen steps unless λ sits almost exactly at 1.
Two consequences you act on. Gradient clipping is not optional for recurrent models — the exploding side arrives abruptly and destroys a run, and clipping by global norm caps the length while preserving direction. And orthogonal initialisation of Whh is the natural choice, because an orthogonal matrix has every singular value exactly 1, which is precisely the knife edge you want to start on.
RNN Unrolled — and LSTM gates controlling information flow
What the LSTM actually changes
The usual explanation is that the cell state is an additive conveyor belt. That is right, and it is worth being precise about why it helps — because the common claim that LSTMs solved vanishing gradients is not true.
$$C_t = f_t \odot C_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde g_t \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\partial C_t}{\partial C_{t-1}} = f_t$$
The gradient along the cell state is a product of forget-gate values rather than of weight matrices. The product has not been eliminated — but the multiplier is now a learned, per-timestep, per-dimension number in (0,1) that the network can drive close to 1 where it needs to remember:
Typical ftOver 100 stepsOver 500 steps
0.990.37 — largely intact0.0066 — fading
0.902.7 × 10⁻⁵effectively zero
Read the second row: at f = 0.9 an LSTM vanishes exactly as fast as a vanilla RNN at λ = 0.9. The architecture does not remove the exponential — it hands you control of its base. That is a large and real improvement, because a gate can learn to stay open on the dimensions that matter. It is also why LSTMs still degrade past a few hundred steps, and why attention — which connects any two positions in a single hop — was a categorical change rather than an incremental one.
It also explains why initialising the forget-gate bias to 1 matters more than it looks. At bias 0 the gate starts at σ(0) = 0.5, so the cell discards half its memory every step before learning anything — a product of 0.5 that vanishes in a handful of steps. At bias 1 it starts at σ(1) ≈ 0.73, the default behaviour becomes remember, and the model only has to learn when to forget, which is the easier direction.
The three gates split the job cleanly: forget decides what to erase from the cell, input decides how much of the new candidate to write, and output decides how much of the cell to expose as this step's hidden state. All three are sigmoids because a gate must be a bounded fraction — one of the few places where a saturating activation is the correct engineering choice rather than a legacy one.
GRU — Gated Recurrent Unit
$$\begin{aligned} z_t = \sigma(W_z[h_{t-1}, x_t]) \quad \text{(update gate)} \\ r_t = \sigma(W_r[h_{t-1}, x_t]) \quad \text{(reset gate)} \\ \tilde{h}_t = \tanh(W[r_t \odot h_{t-1},\, x_t]) \\ h_t = (1-z_t) \odot h_{t-1} + z_t \odot \tilde{h}_t \end{aligned}$$
2 gates instead of 3. No separate cell state. Fewer parameters than LSTM — often matches performance, trains faster.
Bidirectional RNN
Run one RNN forward (x₁→xₙ) and one backward (xₙ→x₁). Concatenate hidden states. Each position sees both past AND future context.
$$\bar{h}_t = \text{concat}(\text{RNN}_{\text{forward}}(x_t),\; \text{RNN}_{\text{backward}}(x_t))$$
Used in BERT's architecture conceptually. Requires full sequence — can't stream. More powerful than unidirectional for NLU tasks.
Parameter counts, derived rather than quoted
The gate structure fixes the size exactly. With hidden size h and input size d, each gate needs a matrix over the concatenation [ht−1, xt], which has width h + d:
$$\text{RNN}: h(h+d) + h \qquad\quad \text{GRU}: 3\big[h(h+d) + h\big] \qquad\quad \text{LSTM}: 4\big[h(h+d) + h\big]$$

GRU is exactly three quarters of an LSTM — not approximately, but by construction, since it has three matrices where the LSTM has four. It merges forget and input into one update gate (what you keep is one minus what you write) and drops the separate cell state, carrying everything in the hidden state.
Which to use: after a decade of comparisons they perform comparably on most tasks. GRU is the better default when compute or memory is constrained; LSTM has a small edge on very long sequences, where separating "what to remember" from "what to expose" pays off. This is not a decision worth agonising over — it matters far less than sequence length, clipping and learning rate.
Why transformers won, and why RNNs did not disappear
The reason is not accuracy in the abstract. It is that ht depends on ht−1, so training cannot be parallelised over time. A length-1,000 sequence means 1,000 strictly sequential steps, each too small to fill a GPU.
RNN / LSTMTransformer
Training — sequential depthO(T) — 1,000 steps at T = 1,000O(1) — all positions at once
Training — total workO(T·h²) — cheaper in raw FLOPsO(T²·h) — 10⁶ pairs at T = 1,000
Path between two positionsO(T) — must pass through every state betweenO(1) — one attention hop
Inference — per tokenO(1) time, O(1) stateO(T) time, O(T) KV cache
Notice that the RNN does less total arithmetic and still loses. On modern hardware, parallelism beats FLOP count — a transformer's 10⁶ pairwise operations are all independent and saturate thousands of cores, while the RNN's 1,000 steps are serial by definition and leave the machine idle. That, plus the O(1) path between any two positions, is the whole story of the transition.
But read the last row. At inference an RNN carries a fixed-size state and costs the same per token forever, while a transformer's cost and memory grow with the context it has already produced. That is why recurrence survives exactly where that property is decisive:
  • Streaming recognition. Real-time speech has to emit output before the utterance ends, so a unidirectional recurrent encoder is a structural fit and a bidirectional or full-attention model is not without modification
  • Edge and microcontroller deployment. A GRU's state is a few hundred numbers, which fits where a KV cache does not. Constant memory per token is what makes kilobyte-scale devices viable
  • Very long or unbounded streams. Sensor and transaction data has no natural end, so an O(T) per-token cost is not merely expensive, it is unusable
And the idea has come back. Modern state-space models — S4, Mamba and relatives — are essentially linear RNNs with structured state, designed so training can be parallelised (via a scan or convolution) while inference keeps the O(1)-per-token recurrent form. They are an explicit attempt to get the transformer's trainability and the RNN's inference profile at once, which is the clearest evidence that recurrence was never the mistake — the non-parallelisable training was.
Training details that decide whether it works
  • Truncated backpropagation through time. You do not backpropagate through a 10,000-step sequence; you cut the graph every k steps, carrying the hidden state forward but not the gradient. Memory is then O(k·h) rather than O(T·h) — and the model structurally cannot learn dependencies longer than k, which is a modelling choice disguised as a memory setting
  • Gradient clipping at global norm 1–5. Mandatory here for the reason in the first card
  • Padding and masking. Batching variable-length sequences requires padding, and the loss must be masked so pad tokens contribute nothing. Unmasked padding teaches the model to predict padding — a silent bug that shows up as suspiciously low loss
  • Sort by length into buckets. Padding a 10-token sequence to 500 wastes almost all the compute. Length-bucketed batching is often the single largest speedup available
  • Bidirectional only where the full sequence exists. A bidirectional model reads the sequence backwards as well as forwards, so it cannot generate left to right and cannot stream. Fine for classification and tagging, impossible for generation
  • Stateful versus stateless. Carrying the hidden state between batches only makes sense if consecutive batches are consecutive in time; otherwise you are initialising each sequence with an unrelated sequence's state
Mistakes that survive code review
  • No gradient clipping. The reused matrix makes explosion abrupt rather than gradual. One bad batch ends the run
  • Claiming LSTMs solved vanishing gradients. They changed the multiplier from a fixed spectral radius to a learned gate. At f = 0.9 the decay is identical to a vanilla RNN's
  • Leaving the forget-gate bias at 0. Starts by discarding half the cell state every step. PyTorch's default is 0, so this is a change you have to make deliberately
  • A bidirectional model for generation. It needs the future to compute the present. Structurally impossible, not merely inaccurate
  • Unmasked padding in the loss. The model learns to predict pad tokens, and the loss looks great
  • Using a vanilla RNN for anything real. It exists to explain the problem that LSTMs and GRUs address. Past about ten steps it does not work
  • Assuming an LSTM will find a long-range dependency. If the signal is 800 steps back, it almost certainly will not, and truncated BPTT may have made it formally impossible. Check your truncation length against the dependency you need
  • Reaching for an RNN on a task with no sequential structure. Tabular rows in arbitrary order are not a sequence, and the recurrence will invent an ordering effect that is not there
  • Ignoring that batch_first is a real source of silent bugs. PyTorch defaults to (sequence, batch, feature). Getting the axes wrong trains a model over the wrong dimension and nothing errors
Where recurrence still ships
  • On-device streaming speech. RNN-Transducer architectures remain standard for real-time transcription because the encoder must consume audio as it arrives and emit text before the utterance ends. Constant per-frame cost and no future context is exactly the recurrent profile
  • Industrial and IoT anomaly detection. GRUs on microcontrollers monitoring sensor streams, where the entire model plus state must fit in kilobytes and run on a battery. Transformer inference is not a candidate at that budget
  • High-frequency financial streams. Constant-time updates per tick with bounded state, on unbounded sequences. The O(1) inference property is the requirement, not an optimisation
  • As the ancestor of the current alternative. The interesting position today is that state-space models reintroduced recurrence deliberately, having made training parallelisable. Understanding why the original recurrence failed — serial training, not the recurrent form — is what makes that development legible rather than surprising
  • What they no longer do: general language modelling, translation, or anything where long-range dependencies and training throughput dominate. Choosing an LSTM there needs a reason beyond familiarity
🎯 In an interview

Explain the RNN failure with the spectral radius. "The same Whh applies every step, so the gradient involves that one matrix to the power t. At λ = 0.9 over 100 steps that is 2.7 × 10⁻⁵; at 1.1 it is 13,781. One number decides, and unlike a deep feed-forward net there is no cancellation across different matrices." That is a sharper answer than "gradients vanish over long sequences".

Then be careful about the LSTM claim, because the careless version is the common answer. The cell state makes ∂Ct/∂Ct−1 = ft, so the product is over learned gate values rather than weight matrices — the exponential is still there but you control its base. At f = 0.99 over 100 steps you retain 0.37; at f = 0.9 you get 2.7 × 10⁻⁵, identical to the vanilla RNN. LSTMs mitigate rather than solve, and that is exactly why attention's O(1) path was a categorical change.

Expect: "Why did transformers replace RNNs?" — Not accuracy first: parallelism. ht needs ht−1, so training is O(T) sequential steps, while a transformer does all positions at once. Note the counter-intuitive part, which is the good bit: the RNN does fewer total FLOPs and still loses, because its work is serial and cannot fill the hardware. Then flip it — at inference the RNN is O(1) per token with O(1) state against the transformer's O(T) and a growing KV cache, which is why streaming ASR and edge devices still use recurrence, and why state-space models are bringing it back.

Volunteer: forget-gate bias to 1 (σ(1) ≈ 0.73, so the default is remember); clipping is mandatory; GRU is exactly 75% of an LSTM's parameters because three matrices instead of four; and truncated BPTT caps the dependency length the model can learn at all, not just the memory it uses.
