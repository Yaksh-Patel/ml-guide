Clustering
The only problem here with no answer key — which means the algorithms cannot tell you when they have failed. Ask k-means for five segments and it returns five, on structured data and on pure noise alike, each one ready for a slide with a persona name attached. The techniques take an afternoon to learn; knowing whether the output means anything is the actual skill, and it is where almost all of the value is.
k-means: what it optimises, and the guarantee it does not give
k-means minimises the within-cluster sum of squares — total squared distance from each point to its assigned centre:
$$\text{WCSS} = \sum_{j=1}^{k}\ \sum_{x \in C_j} \|x - \mu_j\|^2$$
Lloyd's algorithm alternates two steps, each of which provably cannot increase WCSS: assign every point to its nearest centre, then move every centre to the mean of its members. Monotone decrease over a finite number of possible assignments means it always terminates.
  1. Place k initial centres — k-means++ spreads them deliberately rather than uniformly at random
  2. Assign each point to the nearest centre
  3. Recompute each centre as the mean of its assigned points
  4. Repeat until assignments stop changing
it converges — to a local optimum

The global problem is NP-hard, and Lloyd's algorithm only promises it will stop, not that it will stop anywhere good. Different initialisations genuinely land on different answers with different WCSS. This is why n_init exists and is not a formality — sklearn runs the whole thing several times and keeps the best-scoring result. k-means++ reduces how often a bad start happens; it does not remove the need for restarts. If you set n_init=1 to save time, you have swapped a reliable answer for a fast one.

And why does it insist on round clusters? Not by accident of the algorithm — it is the model hiding inside the objective. Minimising squared distance to a centre is exactly fitting a Gaussian mixture with spherical, equal-sized covariances and hard assignments. Spheres are what the objective believes in, so an elongated cluster gets chopped in half and two touching clusters of different sizes get a boundary in the wrong place. Neither is a bug; both are the model being applied where it does not hold.

what this tiny example is really showing

C was exactly equidistant from both initial centres, and after one update it is still nearly so — 2.034 against 2.358. Its cluster membership was decided by an arbitrary tie-break rule and then by a margin of 0.3. The reported clustering is stable; C's membership is not. Nothing in the output tells you which points were near-arbitrary, and on a real dataset a large share of them are. That is what the next card measures.

Silhouette, computed for one point
Everyone quotes silhouette; far fewer can define it. For a single point: let a be its mean distance to the other members of its own cluster, and b its mean distance to the members of the nearest other cluster.
$$s = \frac{b - a}{\max(a,\,b)} \qquad s \in [-1, 1]$$
Take C = (3,4) from the clustering above — own cluster {A, B}, other cluster {D, E}:
$$\begin{aligned} a &= \tfrac{1}{2}\big[d(C,A) + d(C,B)\big] = \tfrac{1}{2}[3.606 + 2.500] = 3.053 \\ b &= \tfrac{1}{2}\big[d(C,D) + d(C,E)\big] = \tfrac{1}{2}[3.606 + 1.118] = 2.362 \\ s_C &= \frac{2.362 - 3.053}{3.053} = \mathbf{-0.226} \end{aligned}$$
Negative — so C is on average closer to the cluster it was not assigned to. Run the same calculation for A and you get a = 2.362, b = 5.964, s = +0.604: comfortably placed. One clustering, one point clearly right and one clearly misassigned, and only the arithmetic distinguishes them.
That is the whole value of silhouette. Near +1 means tight and well-separated. Near 0 means the point sits on a boundary and the clustering is not telling you much. Negative means it is in the wrong cluster. Averaging over all points gives one number for the clustering, but the per-point distribution is far more informative — a mean of 0.4 hiding 20% negative values is a very different situation from a uniform 0.4, and only one of them survives being shown to a stakeholder.
k-means assumes spheres; the data rarely agrees
Choosing k, and why the elbow cannot answer
The elbow method plots WCSS against k and looks for a bend. The problem is structural: WCSS decreases monotonically in k no matter what the data looks like, because more centres can only ever reduce total squared distance. At k = n every point is its own centre and WCSS is exactly 0.
So "inertia dropped when I added a cluster" is never evidence of anything — it is arithmetic. And on genuinely structureless data the curve is a smooth convex sweep with no bend at all, which the method has no way to report. The elbow method cannot express the answer "there are no clusters here", and that is frequently the true answer.
ApproachWhat it actually tells you

The downstream metricdo the clusters differ in something you care about — churn, spend, conversion? If not, the partition is real and useless
In practice k is very often decided outside the data entirely: there are five regional teams, or the campaign budget covers three segments. That is a legitimate answer, and stating it plainly is better than reverse-engineering an elbow to justify a number you had already chosen.
DBSCAN: density instead of distance-to-a-centre
Two parameters, no k. A point is a core point if at least minPts points lie within radius ε of it. Core points within ε of each other join into one cluster, non-core points within ε of a core point join as border points, and anything left over is labelled noise.
  • Finds arbitrary shapes — it grows clusters by connectivity, so crescents and spirals are no harder than blobs
  • Labels outliers explicitly rather than forcing every point into a group. For anomaly detection that label is the output you wanted
  • k is not required, which removes the entire elbow problem
  • Choosing ε: sort every point's distance to its minPts-th neighbour, plot it, and take the knee. Start minPts around 2×(number of dimensions)
the limitation, stated precisely

ε is a single global density threshold. If one genuine cluster is dense and another is diffuse, no single ε works: tighten it and the diffuse cluster becomes noise, loosen it and the dense clusters merge into one. This is not a tuning failure, it is the assumption. HDBSCAN exists specifically to fix it by allowing the threshold to vary and extracting clusters that persist across scales — and it is usually the better default now.

Hierarchical, and the rest of the toolbox
  • Agglomerative: start with every point as its own cluster and repeatedly merge the closest pair, producing a dendrogram. You choose k after looking at the tree, by cutting it — which is a real advantage when you do not know k and want to see the structure at every scale
  • Linkage is the actual decision. Single linkage uses the minimum distance and chains through thin bridges; complete uses the maximum and forces compact blobs; Ward minimises the increase in within-cluster variance and is the sensible default, being the closest hierarchical analogue of k-means
  • O(n²) memory and up to O(n³) time — it does not scale past tens of thousands of points, which is the main reason it is used for exploration rather than production
  • Gaussian Mixture Models are the right step up from k-means when clusters are elliptical or overlapping: full covariances allow stretched shapes, and you get soft assignments with a probability per cluster. BIC or AIC then gives a principled way to pick k, which none of the geometric methods offer
  • Spectral clustering handles connectivity-based structure that defeats centroids, by clustering in the eigenspace of a similarity graph

All the internal metrics share one blind spot: they reward geometry, so they systematically favour whatever shape your algorithm produces. Silhouette will happily prefer k-means' spheres on data whose real clusters are crescents. Never let an internal metric be the only evidence — pair it with a stability check and a downstream outcome.
Mistakes that survive code review
  • Not scaling. Every method here is distance-based, so an unscaled column with a wide range becomes the only column that matters. This is the same 400-million-to-one failure as k-NN, and it is silent
  • Treating the output as discovered truth. The single most consequential error in the topic. k-means on uniform noise returns k tidy, plausible, presentable segments and reports no distress. Validate with silhouette, reseed stability, and a business metric before anyone builds a campaign on it
  • Selecting k from the elbow alone. Inertia falls monotonically; the bend is frequently an artefact of the aspect ratio you plotted. Pair with silhouette and say out loud when k came from the business instead
  • Clustering in high dimensions without reducing first. Distance concentration hits this exactly as it hits k-NN — at 100 raw dimensions "nearest" stops meaning much. PCA or UMAP first, then cluster, and remember that changes what you are clustering
  • k-means on non-spherical data. It will split one elongated cluster and merge two adjacent ones, and the silhouette may still look acceptable. Use DBSCAN/HDBSCAN for shapes, a GMM for ellipses
  • k-means on categorical or one-hot data. A mean of one-hot columns is not a point in the data's space, so the centroid is uninterpretable. Use k-modes, or a Gower distance with a method that accepts a precomputed metric
  • Leaving n_init=1. You are reporting one local optimum and calling it the clustering
  • Scoring clusters with accuracy. There are no labels; cluster ids are arbitrary and permutable. If you have labels, this was a classification problem
  • Forgetting DBSCAN's noise label. Points marked −1 are not cluster −1. Aggregating without filtering silently creates a fake segment out of every outlier in the dataset
What it is actually used for
  • Customer segmentation, with the honest caveat. The most common commercial use and the most commonly oversold. Clusters over RFM or behavioural features give marketing an actionable partition — and the value comes from the segments being different in a metric that matters, not from the algorithm. The right validation is a downstream test: do these groups respond differently to the same campaign?
  • Retrieval and recommendation infrastructure. IVF indexes in vector databases cluster the corpus so a query searches only the nearest few cells. Here clustering is invisible plumbing, and it is judged purely on recall and latency — no interpretation required, which makes it the least controversial use on this list
  • Geospatial density: DBSCAN's home turf. Ride pickups, delivery hotspots, disease case locations. Arbitrary shapes and explicit noise labelling are exactly right for coordinates, where clusters follow streets and coastlines rather than circles
  • Anomaly detection. DBSCAN's noise points and distance-to-nearest-centroid both serve as unsupervised outlier scores — valuable precisely where you have no labelled anomalies to train on
  • Single-cell biology. The canonical pipeline is PCA, then UMAP, then cluster, then annotate clusters as cell types. Notable because the clusters here are checked against known marker genes — external validation, which is what makes the field's use of clustering unusually trustworthy
  • Image and colour quantisation. k-means on pixel colours to reduce a palette is compression with an objective measure of success, and one of the few uses where "is this clustering correct" has a clean answer
🎯 In an interview

The question behind the question is always validation. Say early that clustering always returns clusters, including on noise, so the algorithm choice is the easy part. Then give three checks: silhouette (with the distribution inspected, not just the mean), stability across seeds measured by Adjusted Rand Index, and a downstream business metric that differs between clusters. Candidates who only discuss algorithms sound like they have never shipped a segmentation.

Expect: "How do you choose k?" — Lead with why the elbow is weak: inertia is monotone decreasing in k and hits zero at k = n, so a drop is arithmetic rather than evidence. Silhouette has a real interior maximum and can also report "no structure" by staying near zero. Then say that k is frequently a business constraint, and that naming it as such is more honest than manufacturing an elbow.

Expect: "Why does k-means assume spherical clusters?" — Because minimising squared distance to a centroid is fitting a spherical, equal-variance Gaussian mixture with hard assignments. The assumption is in the objective, not the implementation — which is why no amount of restarting fixes an elongated cluster, and why a GMM with full covariances does.

Be able to compute a silhouette. a = mean distance within your cluster, b = mean distance to the nearest other cluster, s = (b − a)/max(a, b); negative means the point is in the wrong cluster. Do not say "the elbow showed 3" as a complete answer, and do not present a clustering as discovered structure without saying how you checked it.

In code
k-means needs k chosen in advance and finds spheres; DBSCAN finds shapes and labels noise. Running both on the same non-spherical data makes the failure mode obvious.
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans, DBSCAN, AgglomerativeClustering
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score, adjusted_rand_score
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = make_moons(600, noise=.06, random_state=0)
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

for name, model in [('kmeans', KMeans(2, n_init=10, random_state=0)),
                    ('dbscan', DBSCAN(eps=.3, min_samples=5)),
                    ('ward',   AgglomerativeClustering(2, linkage='ward'))]:
    lab = model.fit_predict(X)
    ari = adjusted_rand_score(y, lab)
    sil = silhouette_score(X, lab) if len(set(lab)) > 1 else float('nan')
    print(f"{name:7} ARI={ari:.3f}  silhouette={sil:.3f}  clusters={len(set(lab) - {-1})}")
    # k-means splits each moon in half: it can only draw convex boundaries

# Choosing k when you have no labels: elbow is eyeballed, silhouette is scored
for k in range(2, 7):
    lab = KMeans(k, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(X)
    print(f"  k={k}  inertia={KMeans(k, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X).inertia_:8.1f}"
          f"  silhouette={silhouette_score(X, lab):.3f}")
